Heart Rhythm 2026-10-06 RCT Electrophysiology n = 87 impact 4/5

Efficacy and Safety of Flecainide Versus Amiodarone for the Cardioversion of paroxysmal Atrial Fibrillation in Patients with Coronary Artery Disease without evidence of residual ischemia: The FLECA-ED Randomized Clinical Trial.

Tsioufis PA et al. · PubMed

In 87 emergency department patients with recent-onset paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and coronary artery disease but no residual or active ischemia, intravenous flecainide converted far more patients to sinus rhythm within 6 hours than intravenous amiodarone (85.7% vs 40.0%, P<0.001), with no ventricular proarrhythmia in either arm through 24 hours.

Design
Investigator-initiated, multicenter, open-label, randomized clinical trial with 1:1 allocation (FLECA-ED).
Population
Adults presenting to the emergency department with recent-onset paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and documented coronary artery disease without residual or active ischemia; 87 patients randomized (42 flecainide, 45 amiodarone). Age, sex and other baseline characteristics not reported in the abstract.
Intervention
Intravenous flecainide for pharmacologic cardioversion (n=42). Dose not reported.
Comparison
Intravenous amiodarone (n=45). Dose not reported.
Outcomes
Primary efficacy endpoint: conversion to sinus rhythm within 6 hours, 85.7% with flecainide vs 40.0% with amiodarone (P<0.001); 95% CI not reported. Primary safety endpoint: composite of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, premature ventricular complexes, significant hypotension and bradycardia below 50 bpm within 6 hours; no ventricular proarrhythmic events occurred within 24 hours in either group and the composite rate itself was not reported numerically.

Other results

  • Conversion to sinus rhythm within 6 hours: 85.7% (flecainide) vs 40.0% (amiodarone), P<0.001.
  • Median time to conversion: 20 minutes vs 244 minutes, P<0.001.
  • Hospital admission: 14.3% vs 60.0%, P<0.001.
  • No ventricular proarrhythmic events within 24 hours in either group, with all patients monitored by 24-hour ambulatory ECG.
  • No deaths through 30 days in either group.

Strengths

  • Randomized design addressing a question that has rested on extrapolation from the Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial for decades.
  • Multicenter enrollment in a real emergency department setting, which matches where the decision is actually made.
  • Systematic 24-hour ambulatory ECG monitoring in all patients, so asymptomatic ventricular arrhythmia would be captured rather than missed.
  • Clinically meaningful secondary measures (time to conversion, hospital admission) reported alongside the rhythm endpoint.
  • Enrollment restricted to patients with objectively characterized coronary disease without residual or active ischemia, which defines the population to whom the result might apply.

Limitations

  • Only 87 patients, so the trial is far too small to exclude an uncommon but serious proarrhythmic hazard; the authors themselves call the safety signal hypothesis-generating.
  • Open-label design, which can influence decisions such as hospital admission and the ascertainment of soft endpoints like significant hypotension.
  • Follow-up for the safety composite was only 6 to 24 hours, with mortality to 30 days; later proarrhythmia or recurrence is not addressed.
  • The composite safety endpoint rate and confidence intervals for the primary efficacy difference are not reported in the abstract, so precision cannot be judged.
  • Amiodarone is a slow-acting comparator for acute cardioversion, so superiority on a 6-hour endpoint is partly a function of pharmacokinetics rather than a statement about overall rhythm strategy.
  • Generalizability is limited to patients in whom residual or active ischemia has been excluded; funding source and blinded endpoint adjudication are not reported.

Practice implications

This is the first randomized evidence that intravenous flecainide may be usable for acute cardioversion in selected patients with stable coronary disease and no residual or active ischemia, where it worked faster than amiodarone and cut admissions substantially. It is not yet grounds for abandoning the post-CAST contraindication: with 87 patients and no events, the trial cannot establish safety, and any use outside a trial should be confined to patients in whom ischemia has been objectively excluded, with continuous monitoring. A larger safety-powered trial is needed before guidelines change.

JACC Cardiovasc Interv 2026-10-05 RCT Thrombosis and vascular n = 100 impact 4/5

Mechanical Thrombectomy Versus Anticoagulation Alone in Intermediate-High Risk PE: Improved Functional 90-Day Outcomes in STORM-PE RCT.

Rosovsky RP et al. · PubMed

In this 100-patient randomized trial of intermediate-high risk pulmonary embolism, adding computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy to anticoagulation improved 90-day functional outcomes (6-minute walk distance 478.7 +/- 159.5 m vs 368.0 +/- 127.8 m, P = 0.003) without differences in dyspnea, quality of life, mortality or PE recurrence.

Design
Randomized controlled trial (STORM-PE, NCT05684796), 1:1 allocation across 22 international sites; this report covers functional, quality-of-life and safety outcomes through 90 days. Blinding not reported.
Population
100 patients with acute intermediate-high risk pulmonary embolism; 47 assigned to thrombectomy and 53 to anticoagulation alone. Age, sex and baseline characteristics not reported in the abstract. Median time from randomization to last follow-up visit was 91.0 days (Q1-Q3 80.5-100.0).
Intervention
Computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy (CAVT) plus anticoagulation.
Comparison
Anticoagulation alone.
Outcomes
The trial's primary endpoint was change in right-ventricular to left-ventricular (RV/LV) ratio at 48 hours; its effect estimate, 95% CI and P value are not reported in this abstract. Secondary endpoints reported here: 6-minute walk distance at 90 days, 478.7 +/- 159.5 m with CAVT vs 368.0 +/- 127.8 m with anticoagulation alone (P = 0.003); NYHA Functional Class I at 90 days, 97.4% (37/38) vs 75.6% (34/45) (P = 0.005). Dyspnea and quality-of-life scores were comparable between groups and all-cause mortality, PE-related mortality and symptomatic PE recurrence within 90 days did not differ between arms; the corresponding numbers, 95% CIs and P values are not reported.

Other results

  • 6-minute walk distance at 90 days: 478.7 +/- 159.5 m (CAVT) vs 368.0 +/- 127.8 m (anticoagulation alone), P = 0.003.
  • NYHA Functional Class I at 90 days: 97.4% (37/38) vs 75.6% (34/45), P = 0.005.
  • Test completion was incomplete and similar in both arms: 66.0% (31/47) of CAVT and 64.2% (34/53) of anticoagulation patients completed the 6-minute walk test.
  • Dyspnea and quality-of-life scores were comparable between groups (figures not reported).
  • No differences between arms in all-cause mortality, PE-related mortality or symptomatic PE recurrence within 90 days (figures not reported).
  • Safety rates were described as equivalent; major adverse events within 7 days were a secondary endpoint but are not reported numerically.

Strengths

  • First randomized comparison of a catheter reperfusion strategy against anticoagulation alone in intermediate-high risk PE using patient-centered functional outcomes rather than imaging surrogates alone.
  • Multinational enrollment across 22 sites, which supports applicability beyond a single expert centre.
  • Ninety-day follow-up with objective functional testing (6-minute walk distance) plus NYHA class, addressing the post-PE functional limitation that matters to patients.
  • Prospectively registered trial with mortality, PE recurrence and early major adverse events among the prespecified secondary endpoints.

Limitations

  • Only 100 patients, which is far too small to assess mortality, PE recurrence or procedural safety; the absence of differences in these outcomes is uninformative rather than reassuring.
  • The functional findings are secondary endpoints; the primary 48-hour RV/LV ratio result is not reported in this abstract, so the hierarchy of evidence cannot be judged here.
  • Roughly a third of patients in each arm did not complete the 6-minute walk test (31/47 and 34/53), so the headline walk distances come from selected subsets and are vulnerable to attrition bias.
  • Blinding is not reported, and an open design can influence effort-dependent measures such as 6-minute walk distance and the clinician-assigned NYHA class.
  • Multiple secondary endpoints were examined without any stated adjustment for multiplicity, so the P = 0.003 and P = 0.005 results should be read as exploratory.
  • Dyspnea and quality-of-life scores, which are the more direct patient-reported measures, did not differ, which sits awkwardly with the claimed functional benefit.
  • No confidence intervals are reported for the between-group differences, and the funding source and device-manufacturer involvement are not stated.

Practice implications

STORM-PE provides the first randomized, patient-centered signal that catheter-based thrombectomy may improve 90-day functional recovery in intermediate-high risk PE, and it is worth knowing when counselling a patient about reperfusion. It is not practice-changing on its own: the functional outcomes are secondary, roughly a third of patients did not complete walk testing, patient-reported dyspnea and quality of life did not differ, and 100 patients cannot speak to mortality or procedural harm. Reserve thrombectomy for patients in whom a PE response team judges the risk-benefit favourable, and await adequately powered trials with hard clinical endpoints before broadening its use.

Eur Heart J 2026-10-06 RCT Electrophysiology n = 82 impact 3/5

Biventricular vs conduction system pacing after atrioventricular node ablation for heart failure with atrial fibrillation: the CONDUCT-AF trial.

Žižek D et al. · PubMed

In 82 patients with refractory atrial fibrillation and heart failure undergoing atrioventricular node ablation, conduction system pacing with left bundle branch area pacing was non-inferior to biventricular pacing for 6-month LVEF improvement (between-group difference 0.4%; 95% CI, -3.1 to 3.8; P = .002 for non-inferiority), with shorter paced QRS and shorter procedural and fluoroscopy times.

Design
Investigator-initiated, multicentre, randomized non-inferiority trial (CONDUCT-AF) across 10 European centres, 1:1 allocation, intention-to-treat analysis, with the primary echocardiographic endpoint adjudicated by an independent core laboratory. Patient and operator blinding not reported; the non-inferiority margin is not reported.
Population
82 randomized patients with refractory atrial fibrillation, heart failure with LVEF below 50%, narrow QRS duration of 120 ms or less, and NT-proBNP above 600 ng/L, all undergoing atrioventricular node ablation. Mean age 72 +/- 7 years, 46% male. Allocation numbers per arm are not reported.
Intervention
Conduction system pacing using left bundle branch area pacing.
Comparison
Biventricular pacing.
Outcomes
Primary endpoint, change in LVEF at 6 months by independent core laboratory: LVEF rose from 36% (95% CI, 33-39) to 46% (95% CI, 44-49) with conduction system pacing (P < .001) and from 34% (95% CI, 31-36) to 46% (95% CI, 43-49) with biventricular pacing (P < .001); between-group difference 0.4% (95% CI, -3.1 to 3.8; P = .002 for non-inferiority). Secondary endpoints: paced QRS mean difference -14 ms (95% CI, -19 to -9; P < .001) favouring conduction system pacing, and significantly shorter procedural and fluoroscopy times with conduction system pacing (figures not reported). Changes in left ventricular volumes, clinical parameters, and the composite of worsening heart failure or cardiovascular death were comparable between groups (figures, 95% CIs and P values not reported).

Other results

  • Primary endpoint between-group LVEF difference at 6 months: 0.4% (95% CI, -3.1 to 3.8; P = .002 for non-inferiority).
  • LVEF with conduction system pacing improved from 36% (95% CI, 33-39) to 46% (95% CI, 44-49), P < .001.
  • LVEF with biventricular pacing improved from 34% (95% CI, 31-36) to 46% (95% CI, 43-49), P < .001.
  • Paced QRS was shorter with conduction system pacing: mean difference -14 ms (95% CI, -19 to -9; P < .001).
  • Procedural and fluoroscopy times were significantly shorter with conduction system pacing (durations not reported).
  • Left ventricular volumes, clinical parameters and the composite of worsening heart failure or cardiovascular death were comparable between groups (figures not reported).

Strengths

  • Randomized head-to-head comparison in a setting where the choice between conduction system and biventricular pacing has rested largely on observational data and operator preference.
  • Multicentre design across 10 European centres, so the result is not a single-operator phenomenon.
  • The primary echocardiographic endpoint was read by an independent core laboratory, which protects the main outcome from the most likely source of measurement bias in an unblinded pacing trial.
  • Intention-to-treat analysis, with a well-defined entry phenotype (LVEF below 50%, QRS 120 ms or less, NT-proBNP above 600 ng/L) that matches the patients in whom this decision actually arises.
  • Both arms showed a large and similar absolute LVEF gain of roughly 10 to 12 points, confirming that ablate-and-pace itself works in this population.
  • Procedural advantages (shorter paced QRS, shorter procedure and fluoroscopy time) are concrete and favour the less invasive implant.

Limitations

  • Only 82 patients, so the trial is powered for a surrogate endpoint and cannot address worsening heart failure, hospitalization or death; the statement that clinical outcomes were comparable is uninformative at this sample size.
  • The primary endpoint is 6-month LVEF change, a surrogate; durability of lead performance and reverse remodelling beyond 6 months is not addressed.
  • The non-inferiority margin is not reported, so the clinical meaning of P = .002 for non-inferiority cannot be judged; the confidence interval for the difference (-3.1 to 3.8) nevertheless excludes any large LVEF penalty.
  • Blinding of patients, implanting operators and clinical-endpoint assessors is not reported; only the echocardiographic core laboratory is described as independent.
  • Allocation numbers per arm, crossover or bailout rates, and lead revision or threshold data are not reported, which matters because left bundle branch area pacing leads are relatively new.
  • Baseline LVEF differed slightly between arms (36% vs 34%), and with 41 or so patients per group small imbalances can influence a change-from-baseline endpoint.
  • Numerical values for the secondary endpoints (volumes, clinical parameters, composite events, procedure and fluoroscopy times) are not reported in the abstract, so their magnitude and precision cannot be appraised.
  • Enrollment was confined to 10 European centres with presumed conduction system pacing expertise, limiting generalizability to lower-volume implanters; funding source is not reported.

Practice implications

For a patient with refractory atrial fibrillation, reduced LVEF and a narrow QRS who is going for atrioventricular node ablation, this trial supports left bundle branch area pacing as a reasonable default instead of a biventricular system: LVEF recovery was equivalent, paced QRS was 14 ms narrower, and the procedure was quicker with less fluoroscopy. The caveats are that the endpoint is a 6-month surrogate in 82 patients, so equivalence for heart failure events and death is unproven, and long-term left bundle lead performance is not addressed here. Reasonable practice is to offer conduction system pacing where operator expertise exists, with biventricular pacing retained as the fallback when lead placement fails, and to keep following these patients for lead integrity.

JACC Heart Fail 2026-10-09 RCT Heart failure n = 171 impact 3/5

Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Detection and Management of Physiological Instability in Heart Failure: Results of the Randomized LINK-HF2 Trial.

Sideris K et al. · PubMed

In this 171-patient randomized feasibility trial after heart failure hospitalization, AI-guided wearable monitoring with structured clinician notification was feasible and reduced AI-detected physiological instability (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.60-0.97; P = 0.025) but did not reduce heart failure rehospitalization (16.3% vs 18.7%; HR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.43-1.84; P = 0.76).

Design
Prospective, randomized, open-label, blinded-endpoint (PROBE) implementation and feasibility study (LINK-HF2, NCT04502563) at 5 Veterans Affairs medical centers. Implementation outcomes were feasibility, acceptability and usability; all clinical endpoints were explicitly exploratory and compared with Cox proportional hazards models.
Population
171 analyzed adults enrolled at discharge after hospitalization for acute heart failure; 80 (46.8%) assigned to intervention and 91 (53.2%) to control. Age, sex, ejection fraction and other baseline characteristics not reported in the abstract. All participants wore a continuous multisensor patch for up to 90 days.
Intervention
AI-guided monitoring with clinician notification: a personalized similarity-based model detected deviations in cardiorespiratory and activity signals from the multisensor patch and generated notifications, which triggered a structured clinician response algorithm.
Comparison
Standard care; control participants also wore the continuous multisensor patch but their data did not generate clinician notifications.
Outcomes
This was a feasibility study, so implementation outcomes (feasibility, acceptability, usability) were primary; the main exploratory clinical endpoint was heart failure rehospitalization, which occurred in 13 (16.3%) intervention versus 17 (18.7%) control patients (HR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.43-1.84; P = 0.76). The secondary exploratory composite of all-cause unplanned rehospitalization, emergency department visits and all-cause mortality did not differ (HR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.63-1.70; P = 0.89). AI-detected physiological instability was lower in the intervention group (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.60-0.97; P = 0.025).

Other results

  • Heart failure rehospitalization: 13 (16.3%) intervention vs 17 (18.7%) control; HR, 0.90 (95% CI, 0.43-1.84); P = 0.76.
  • Composite of all-cause unplanned rehospitalization, emergency department visits and all-cause mortality: HR, 1.03 (95% CI, 0.63-1.70); P = 0.89.
  • AI-detected physiological instability: HR, 0.76 (95% CI, 0.60-0.97); P = 0.025, favouring the intervention.
  • Median monitoring adherence was 72.4% of follow-up days.
  • Clinicians responded to communicated notifications within a median of 1 day (range, 1-5 days).
  • Qualitative analysis showed high patient acceptance and clinician engagement; workflow integration and response uncertainty were the main implementation barriers.

Strengths

  • Randomized rather than single-arm, which is unusual for a digital-health feasibility study and avoids the before-after confounding that plagues remote monitoring literature.
  • Blinded endpoint assessment (PROBE design) mitigates ascertainment bias for rehospitalization in a trial where the intervention cannot be masked.
  • Control patients also wore the patch, so the comparison isolates the effect of acting on the AI notifications rather than the effect of wearing a sensor.
  • Mixed-methods approach: hard adherence and response-time metrics plus qualitative work that identifies why the intervention did or did not translate, which is the genuinely useful output of a feasibility study.
  • Multicentre enrollment across 5 Veterans Affairs sites in the high-risk postdischarge window, a clinically realistic setting.
  • Honest framing: the authors label the clinical endpoints exploratory and draw the right inference, that detection without a trusted, mechanistically matched therapeutic response does not change outcomes.

Limitations

  • A 171-patient feasibility study is nowhere near powered for clinical endpoints; the rehospitalization confidence interval (0.43-1.84) spans a halving to near-doubling of risk, so no conclusion about efficacy is possible in either direction.
  • All clinical endpoints were exploratory with multiple comparisons and no multiplicity adjustment, so the single nominally significant result (AI-detected instability, P = 0.025) should not be over-read.
  • That significant endpoint is the algorithm's own output rather than a patient-relevant outcome, and it is partly circular: the intervention arm was the one in which clinicians acted to suppress exactly those deviations.
  • Median adherence of only 72.4% of follow-up days caps any achievable benefit and reflects real-world wearable attrition.
  • Open-label design means intervention patients received additional clinical attention beyond the notifications themselves, which cannot be separated from the algorithm's contribution.
  • A Veterans Affairs population is predominantly older and male, limiting generalizability; baseline characteristics including ejection fraction and background guideline-directed therapy are not reported.
  • Monitoring lasted up to 90 days with follow-up duration not clearly stated, and no longer-term outcomes are available.
  • Funding source and device-manufacturer involvement are not reported.

Practice implications

This does not support adopting AI-guided wearable monitoring after heart failure discharge as routine care. The trial shows the technology is deployable in a real health system with acceptable adherence and prompt clinician response, and that acting on notifications suppresses the algorithm's own instability signal, but there was no hint of fewer rehospitalizations or deaths, and the trial was not built to detect one. Its most transferable lesson is about workflow: clinicians cited response uncertainty as a main barrier, so any future programme needs a prespecified, evidence-based action for each alert rather than a notification alone. Treat remote AI monitoring as investigational pending an adequately powered outcome trial.

JAMA Cardiol 2026-10-07 Meta-analysis Hypertension n = 88,830 impact 4/5

Intensive vs Standard Blood Pressure Lowering in People With and Without Chronic Kidney Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.

Iatridi F et al. · PubMed

In 27 trials with 88 830 participants, intensive blood pressure lowering reduced major cardiovascular events by 15% per 10-mm Hg lower systolic BP (RR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.81-0.89) with no heterogeneity by CKD status, and although it increased sustained eGFR declines it did not increase kidney failure (RR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.84-1.11); because event rates are higher in CKD, absolute benefit was at least as large there.

Design
Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials (PRISMA-reported). MEDLINE and Embase searched from inception to January 16, 2026. Trial-level relative risks were standardized to a 10-mm Hg lower achieved systolic BP by multiplying each trial's log RR and standard error by 10/d, then pooled by inverse variance weighting. CKD versus non-CKD effects were compared by chi-square heterogeneity tests (1-tailed), with chi-square tests for trend across achieved SBP differences. Risk of bias assessed with Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 2; publication bias by funnel plots and the Egger test. Analyses in R 4.4.3.
Population
27 parallel-group trials comprising 41 reports and 88 830 adults randomized to specified intensive versus standard BP targets, with at least 1 year of intervention and follow-up; average within-trial follow-up 1 to 8.4 years. Fourteen trials provided CKD data (1142 cardiovascular events among 11 103 patients; 422 kidney failure events among 5855 patients) and 19 trials provided non-CKD data (4498 cardiovascular events among 76 785 participants; 158 kidney failure events among 31 647 participants). Excluded: trials of non-BP-target interventions, trials randomizing to drugs rather than targets, crossover or cluster trials, and trials in hospitalized, pregnant, dialysis or acute-setting patients. Achieved SBP reduction was similar in CKD and non-CKD populations (11.1 vs 11.2 mm Hg). Trial-level achieved between-group SBP differences ranged from 1.3 to 15.4 mm Hg. Intensive SBP targets were most often less than 120 mm Hg (7 of 27, 26%) or less than 130 mm Hg (7 of 27, 26%); 4 trials used diastolic BP and 2 used mean arterial pressure targets. Sixteen studies were at low risk of bias and 11 had some concerns.
Intervention
Randomized allocation to an intensive BP target, with effects standardized per 10-mm Hg lower achieved systolic BP.
Comparison
Randomized allocation to a standard (less intensive) BP target in the same trials.
Outcomes
Primary outcome, major cardiovascular events as defined by each trial (5640 events in 24 trials): RR per 10-mm Hg lower SBP 0.85 (95% CI, 0.81-0.89) overall; 0.89 (95% CI, 0.81-0.99) in CKD populations versus 0.84 (95% CI, 0.80-0.88) in non-CKD populations, CKD versus non-CKD heterogeneity P = .26. Within-subgroup heterogeneity by Cochran Q: CKD P = .65, non-CKD P = .01. Key secondary outcome, kidney failure (580 events in 11 trials, 37 502 participants): RR per 10-mm Hg lower SBP 0.97 (95% CI, 0.84-1.11), CKD versus non-CKD heterogeneity P = .77; within-subgroup Cochran Q CKD P = .67, non-CKD P = .84. Combining within-trial and posttrial follow-up (1331 outcomes): RR 0.93 (95% CI, 0.84-1.02).

Other results

  • Major cardiovascular events: RR per 10-mm Hg lower SBP 0.85 (95% CI, 0.81-0.89); 5640 events in 24 trials.
  • No heterogeneity by CKD status for cardiovascular events: CKD RR 0.89 (95% CI, 0.81-0.99) vs non-CKD RR 0.84 (95% CI, 0.80-0.88), P = .26.
  • Estimated absolute cardiovascular benefit overall was 3.0 (SE 0.4) outcomes avoided per 1000 person-years; by subgroup it was 4.2 (SE 1.2) in CKD vs 2.7 (SE 0.4) in non-CKD per 1000 person-years, P = .24. (The published abstract gives the CKD figure as 4.3 [1.2]; the full text reports 4.2 [1.2].)
  • Kidney failure: no effect, RR per 10-mm Hg lower SBP 0.97 (95% CI, 0.84-1.11); and RR 0.93 (95% CI, 0.84-1.02) combining within-trial and posttrial data (1331 outcomes).
  • Sustained eGFR declines were increased: 30% or greater decline RR 1.42 (95% CI, 1.28-1.58) and 40% or greater decline RR 1.47 (95% CI, 1.30-1.66) per 10-mm Hg lower SBP.
  • No significant increase in 50% or greater sustained eGFR decline (RR 1.29; 95% CI, 0.94-1.76) or in the composite of doubling of serum creatinine or kidney failure (RR 0.97; 95% CI, 0.83-1.14).
  • With posttrial follow-up, doubling of serum creatinine appeared increased (RR 1.15; 95% CI, 1.04-1.27; 4 trials) without an increase in kidney failure (RR 0.93; 95% CI, 0.84-1.02; 12 trials).
  • Exploratory all-cause mortality (2737 deaths in 24 trials): RR per 10-mm Hg lower SBP 0.89 (95% CI, 0.83-0.95), with no evidence of heterogeneity by CKD status.
  • Cardiovascular events in CKD were concentrated in a few trials: 819 of 1142 events (72%) came from 4 trials (SPRINT-CKD 254 [22%], ACCORD-CKD 240 [21%], HOT-CKD 176 [15%], AASK 149 [13%]); similarly 487 of 580 kidney failure events (84%) came from AASK, MDRD, ACCORD and REIN-2.
  • Sensitivity analyses without standardization and excluding trials with fewer than 500 participants gave similar results.

Strengths

  • Large and current evidence base: 27 randomized trials and 88 830 participants, incorporating three recently reported large trials (ESPRIT n = 11 255, BPROAD n = 12 821, STEP n = 8511) that add 32 587 participants including 1843 with CKD, and increasing CKD cardiovascular events by more than 30% over the previous Cochrane review (1142 vs 693).
  • Restriction to trials randomizing to BP targets rather than to specific drugs isolates the effect of BP lowering itself rather than off-target drug effects.
  • Standardizing each trial to a 10-mm Hg achieved SBP difference addresses the wide between-trial variation in achieved separation (1.3 to 15.4 mm Hg) and makes CKD and non-CKD comparisons interpretable; achieved reduction was near-identical in the two populations (11.1 vs 11.2 mm Hg).
  • Analysing the whole trial population before testing for CKD effect modification is methodologically sounder than a CKD-only synthesis, and it explains why this analysis differs from the Cochrane review that found no cardiovascular benefit in CKD (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.87-1.15).
  • Duplicate independent screening, extraction and formal Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 assessment, with unpublished CKD subgroup data obtained directly from the MDRD, AASK and ESPRIT investigators.
  • Kidney failure, a hard but late-accruing endpoint, was strengthened by adding up to 10 years of posttrial follow-up from 5 trials, giving 1331 events rather than 580.
  • Prespecified sensitivity analyses (unstandardized estimates, excluding trials under 500 participants) and funnel plot assessment were all concordant with the main results.

Limitations

  • Trial-level (tabular) rather than individual participant data, so the authors could not analyse components of the composite cardiovascular endpoint or subgroups by diabetes status, eGFR category or albuminuria.
  • Residual uncertainty specifically for eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, where trial data remain sparse; this is precisely the group in which guidelines disagree.
  • Some participants counted as non-CKD probably had occult CKD, which would dilute any true CKD versus non-CKD difference and reduce power for the heterogeneity tests; the P = .26 interaction is therefore weak evidence of homogeneity rather than proof of it.
  • Each trial's own definition of major cardiovascular events was used, and trials differed in BP measurement method and in whether systolic, diastolic or mean arterial pressure was targeted, adding heterogeneity (non-CKD Cochran Q P = .01).
  • The standardization method assumes a log-linear BP-outcome relationship and that all benefit is mediated by BP lowering alone; it also means the headline RR is a per-10-mm Hg construct rather than the effect of any single target.
  • Evidence is dominated by a handful of trials: 4 trials supplied 72% of CKD cardiovascular events and 84% of kidney failure events, so the pooled estimates are not as independent as the trial count suggests.
  • Funnel plots showed some asymmetry for cardiovascular events (though the formal test was nonsignificant), raising the possibility of small-study or publication effects.
  • Harms other than kidney endpoints were not meta-analysed here; the increase in acute kidney injury seen in SPRINT (204/4678 [4.4%] vs 120/4683 [2.6%]; HR, 1.71; P < .001) and the hypotension, syncope and fall risks noted from other meta-analyses are discussed rather than quantified.
  • One-tailed P values for heterogeneity and no prespecified significance threshold, with many outcomes examined and no multiplicity adjustment, so the secondary kidney findings are best read as exploratory.
  • All-cause mortality was explicitly exploratory, and funding sources for this meta-analysis are not reported in the material provided.

Practice implications

For cardiovascular risk reduction, CKD is not a reason to accept a higher BP target: the relative benefit per 10-mm Hg is the same as in people without CKD, and because CKD patients have more events the absolute gain is at least as large (4.2 vs 2.7 events avoided per 1000 person-years). Mortality moved in the same direction (RR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.83-0.95). The kidney signal needs the right interpretation: intensive treatment increases 30% and 40% sustained eGFR declines, plausibly a haemodynamic effect of the extra diuretic and calcium-channel blocker use needed to reach lower targets, and it did not translate into more kidney failure across 1331 events including long-term posttrial follow-up. Practically, pursue an intensive target in CKD rather than backing off, but monitor BP, eGFR and electrolytes closely after each intensification, do not abandon the strategy for an expected early eGFR dip, and stay alert for acute kidney injury, hypotension, syncope and falls. Uncertainty remains for eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, where dedicated trials are still needed.

JACC Clin Electrophysiol 2026-09-26 Meta-analysis Electrophysiology n = 1,487 impact 3/5

Adjunctive Low-Voltage Area Ablation Reduces Atrial Fibrillation Recurrence in Persistent Atrial Fibrillation: A Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis, and Trial Sequential Analysis.

Virk SA et al. · PubMed

Pooling 7 randomized trials and 1,487 patients with persistent atrial fibrillation, adding low-voltage area ablation to pulmonary vein isolation reduced recurrent AF or atrial tachyarrhythmia at 12 months or more (HR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.50-0.88; I2 = 55%; P = 0.004) at the cost of about 30 minutes of extra procedure time and with no excess in major complications.

Design
Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with trial sequential analysis. Electronic databases searched from inception to September 2026. Random-effects models were used for pooling. The reporting guideline followed, number of reviewers screening and extracting, and formal risk-of-bias assessment are not reported.
Population
7 randomized controlled trials comprising 1,487 participants with persistent atrial fibrillation; weighted mean age 68 years, 60% male. Numbers allocated to each strategy, follow-up durations of individual trials, and arrhythmia monitoring methods are not reported.
Intervention
Pulmonary vein isolation plus adjunctive low-voltage area ablation, that is electroanatomically guided substrate modification targeting areas of low bipolar voltage.
Comparison
Pulmonary vein isolation alone.
Outcomes
Primary endpoint, recurrent AF or atrial tachyarrhythmia at 12 months or more after the index ablation: HR, 0.67 (95% CI, 0.50-0.88; I2 = 55%; P = 0.004) favouring pulmonary vein isolation plus low-voltage area ablation. Secondary endpoints: major periprocedural complications RR, 1.12 (95% CI, 0.60-2.10; P = 0.7); total procedure duration mean difference 30.5 minutes (95% CI, 5.8-55.2; P = 0.02); radiofrequency ablation duration mean difference 12.3 minutes (95% CI, 5.4-19.1; P < 0.001); fluoroscopy time not increased (figures not reported).

Other results

  • Recurrent AF or atrial tachyarrhythmia at 12 months or more: HR, 0.67 (95% CI, 0.50-0.88; I2 = 55%; P = 0.004).
  • Trial sequential analysis showed the cumulative Z-curve crossed the monitoring boundary for benefit, supporting robustness of the effect.
  • Major periprocedural complications: RR, 1.12 (95% CI, 0.60-2.10; P = 0.7), that is no detectable excess.
  • Total procedure duration increased by a mean of 30.5 minutes (95% CI, 5.8-55.2; P = 0.02).
  • Radiofrequency ablation duration increased by a mean of 12.3 minutes (95% CI, 5.4-19.1; P < 0.001).
  • Fluoroscopy time was not increased (figures not reported).
  • Evidence base: 7 randomized trials, 1,487 participants, weighted mean age 68 years, 60% male.

Strengths

  • Restricted to randomized trials, so the comparison is not vulnerable to the selection bias that dominates observational substrate-modification literature.
  • Addresses a genuinely contested question, since the individual trials of low-voltage area ablation have been inconsistent and individually underpowered.
  • Primary endpoint required at least 12 months of follow-up after the index procedure, which is long enough to be clinically meaningful for an ablation outcome.
  • Trial sequential analysis adds information beyond a conventional pooled estimate by indicating whether the cumulative evidence has crossed a monitoring boundary rather than reflecting repeated testing of an accruing literature.
  • Random-effects pooling is the appropriate choice given real differences in mapping protocols and ablation technique between trials.
  • Both efficacy and the practical costs of the strategy (procedure time, radiofrequency time, fluoroscopy, complications) are reported, so the trade-off is explicit.

Limitations

  • Substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 55%) for the primary endpoint, which is unsurprising given that trials differ in voltage cut-offs, mapping rhythm and catheters, and which means the single pooled hazard ratio may not apply to any particular protocol.
  • Only 7 trials and 1,487 patients, so the pooled estimate rests on a small evidence base and the confidence interval (0.50-0.88) still admits anything from a large to a modest benefit.
  • No formal risk-of-bias assessment, PROSPERO registration or publication-bias evaluation is reported, and blinding is inherently difficult in ablation trials; outcome ascertainment depends on monitoring intensity that is not described.
  • Trial sequential analysis is sensitive to the assumed control event rate and relative risk reduction used to set the monitoring boundary, none of which are reported here, so the robustness claim cannot be independently checked.
  • The complication relative risk of 1.12 (95% CI, 0.60-2.10) is too imprecise to establish safety; a clinically important excess of tamponade, phrenic injury or atrio-oesophageal injury is not excluded.
  • Only arrhythmia recurrence is addressed; no data on quality of life, repeat ablation, heart failure events, stroke or mortality are reported.
  • The included trials predate or do not isolate pulsed field ablation, so applicability to contemporary practice where energy source and lesion durability are changing is uncertain.
  • Study-level rather than individual participant data, so the key clinical question of which patients (by low-voltage area extent, atrial size or AF duration) benefit cannot be answered; funding and conflicts are not reported.

Practice implications

For patients with persistent atrial fibrillation, this is the strongest synthesis yet that mapping and ablating low-voltage areas on top of pulmonary vein isolation cuts arrhythmia recurrence by roughly a third, and it costs about half an hour of extra procedure time without an evident safety penalty. It is reasonable to offer adjunctive low-voltage area ablation in centres with electroanatomic mapping expertise, particularly in patients with a high expected recurrence risk. Two cautions temper enthusiasm: the 55% heterogeneity means the voltage threshold and mapping protocol matter and the best one is undefined, and the safety estimate is too imprecise to be reassuring. This should not yet be treated as a mandatory step for every persistent AF ablation, and the strategy needs re-testing in the era of pulsed field ablation.

Role of Lifestyle and Risk Factor Modification Clinics in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.

Zhao Y et al. · PubMed

Pooling 11 randomized trials and 3,364 patients with atrial fibrillation, lifestyle and risk factor modification clinics cut arrhythmia recurrence after catheter ablation (OR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.23-0.51; P<0.001; I2=0%), reduced AF-related hospitalization (OR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.51-0.98; P=0.04) and improved quality of life, with no detected effect on heart failure hospitalization, cardiovascular death or stroke.

Design
Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials comparing lifestyle and risk factor modification clinics with usual care. Databases searched, search dates, reporting guideline, registration, number of reviewers, pooling model (fixed versus random effects), risk-of-bias assessment and publication-bias evaluation are not reported.
Population
11 randomized controlled trials with 3,364 patients with atrial fibrillation, of which 5 trials were conducted in the context of AF ablation. Mean age ranged from 58 to 73 years, 30% were female and 18% had persistent AF. Follow-up ranged from 3 to 24 months. Numbers randomized to each arm are not reported.
Intervention
Care in a lifestyle and risk factor modification clinic: a comprehensive, multidisciplinary, patient-centered programme addressing modifiable AF risk factors. The specific components, intensity and duration of the programmes are not reported.
Comparison
Usual care.
Outcomes
Primary endpoint, atrial arrhythmia recurrence: OR, 0.34 (95% CI, 0.23-0.51; P<0.001; I2=0%) in the comparison after catheter ablation. Secondary endpoints: AF-related hospitalization OR, 0.70 (95% CI, 0.51-0.98; P=0.04; I2=21%); quality of life, mean improvement on the Short Form 36 Questionnaire 8.90 (95% CI, 6.91-10.90; P<0.001); heart failure-related hospitalization no detected difference (P=0.16); cardiovascular death no detected difference (P=0.79); stroke or transient ischemic attack no detected difference (P=0.83). Effect estimates and confidence intervals for the three null secondary endpoints are not reported.

Other results

  • Atrial arrhythmia recurrence after catheter ablation: OR, 0.34 (95% CI, 0.23-0.51); P<0.001; I2=0%.
  • AF-related hospitalization: OR, 0.70 (95% CI, 0.51-0.98); P=0.04; I2=21%.
  • Quality of life: mean improvement on Short Form 36 of 8.90 (95% CI, 6.91-10.90); P<0.001.
  • Heart failure-related hospitalization: no detected difference, P=0.16 (effect estimate not reported).
  • Cardiovascular death: no detected difference, P=0.79 (effect estimate not reported).
  • Stroke or transient ischemic attack: no detected difference, P=0.83 (effect estimate not reported).
  • Evidence base: 11 randomized trials, 3,364 patients, 5 trials in the ablation setting, follow-up 3 to 24 months, 30% female, 18% persistent AF.

Strengths

  • Confined to randomized trials, which matters particularly for lifestyle interventions where motivated self-selection otherwise inflates apparent benefit.
  • Reasonable aggregate size for this literature (3,364 patients across 11 trials) on a question usually answered by single underpowered studies.
  • Zero statistical heterogeneity for the primary endpoint (I2=0%) and low heterogeneity for hospitalization (I2=21%), so the pooled estimates are internally consistent despite the trials differing in programme design.
  • Effect size for recurrence is large (OR, 0.34) and the confidence interval is tight, so the direction of benefit after ablation is not in doubt.
  • Quality of life was analysed with a single instrument (Short Form 36), and the improvement of 8.90 points exceeds what is usually considered clinically meaningful on that scale.
  • Reports the null secondary endpoints rather than only the favourable ones, which keeps the overall picture honest.
  • Separating the ablation and non-ablation settings is clinically appropriate, since recurrence means different things in each.

Limitations

  • Methodological reporting is thin: no search dates, pooling model, registration, formal risk-of-bias assessment or publication-bias assessment is described, so the quality of the synthesis cannot be appraised.
  • The primary endpoint result is specific to the 5 trials performed around catheter ablation, not to the whole 11-trial, 3,364-patient population, so the headline effect rests on a much smaller subset whose size is not reported.
  • Lifestyle interventions cannot be blinded, and arrhythmia recurrence and quality of life are both susceptible to differential ascertainment and expectation effects in an open design; the degree of rhythm monitoring in each trial is not reported.
  • What a lifestyle and risk factor modification clinic actually consists of is heterogeneous and not described, so the result does not specify a reproducible intervention; it may reflect weight loss, blood pressure or sleep apnoea treatment, exercise, alcohol reduction, better anticoagulation adherence, or simply more clinical contact.
  • Follow-up as short as 3 months in some trials is inadequate for recurrence or hospitalization endpoints, and durability beyond 24 months is unknown.
  • The trials are too small and too short to assess stroke, cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization; those null results are uninformative rather than evidence of no effect, and no effect estimates or confidence intervals are given.
  • The AF-related hospitalization result is borderline (upper confidence limit 0.98, P=0.04) among several secondary endpoints with no stated multiplicity adjustment.
  • Study-level data only, so no identification of which patients or which risk factors drive the benefit; 18% persistent AF and 30% women limit applicability to those groups, and funding and conflicts are not reported.

Practice implications

This supports building structured risk factor management into AF care rather than treating rhythm control as a procedural problem alone. The strongest case is around catheter ablation, where referral to a multidisciplinary lifestyle and risk factor clinic was associated with roughly a two-thirds reduction in the odds of recurrence, alongside fewer AF hospitalizations and a clinically meaningful quality-of-life gain. Practically, that means systematically addressing weight, blood pressure, alcohol, sleep apnoea, glycaemia and physical activity before and after ablation, resourced as a service rather than left to advice in clinic. Two limits should be kept in view: the trials do not define which programme components are doing the work, and they are far too small and short to claim any effect on stroke or mortality, so these clinics complement rather than replace anticoagulation and guideline-directed therapy.

Incidence, Process of Care, and Outcomes of In-Hospital Cardiac Arrest in China: A Multicenter Prospective Registry Study.

Wang C et al. · PubMed

In the first large multicentre Chinese registry of in-hospital cardiac arrest (17,602 adults at 37 hospitals), incidence was 4.1 per 1000 admissions and only 9.1% survived to 30 days or discharge (7.1% with a favourable neurological outcome), despite 95.9% receiving CPR within 1 minute; post-resuscitation care was the conspicuous gap, with temperature control in 3.6% and coronary angiography in 5.2% of those achieving return of spontaneous circulation.

Design
Multicentre prospective registry cohort study (BASIC-IHCA: Baseline Investigation of In-Hospital Cardiac Arrest). Descriptive; there is no comparator arm and no adjusted analysis. Enrolment from July 1, 2019 to December 31, 2020 at 37 hospitals across 29 provinces in China.
Population
17,602 adults (aged 18 years or older) who experienced in-hospital cardiac arrest and received cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Median age 66 years, 65.5% male. Cardiac etiology in 6,569 (37.3%); initial shockable rhythm in 2,380 (13.5%).
Intervention
No intervention was assigned; the exposure is in-hospital cardiac arrest with attempted resuscitation, with process-of-care measures (timeliness of resuscitation response and use of post-resuscitation interventions) described.
Comparison
None; this is a single-cohort descriptive registry. The implicit comparison is with published outcomes from high-income countries, but no formal comparison is reported.
Outcomes
Primary outcome, survival to 30 days or hospital discharge: 1,610 of 17,602 patients (9.1%). Secondary outcomes: sustained return of spontaneous circulation 6,200 (35.2%); favourable neurological outcome (Cerebral Performance Category 1-2) at 30 days or discharge 1,241 (7.1%); alive at 12 months 1,075 (6.1%); favourable neurological outcome at 12 months 986 (5.6%). Six-month survival was a stated secondary outcome but is not reported numerically. No effect estimates, confidence intervals or P values are reported, as the study is descriptive.

Other results

  • Annual incidence of adult in-hospital cardiac arrest: 4.1 per 1000 admissions.
  • Survival to 30 days or hospital discharge: 1,610 of 17,602 (9.1%); favourable neurological outcome 1,241 (7.1%).
  • Sustained return of spontaneous circulation: 6,200 (35.2%).
  • Survival at 12 months: 1,075 (6.1%); favourable neurological outcome at 12 months 986 (5.6%).
  • Cardiopulmonary resuscitation within 1 minute: 16,872 patients (95.9%).
  • Timely defibrillation in 71.4% of patients with an initial shockable rhythm and timely epinephrine in 84.4% of those with a nonshockable rhythm.
  • Among the 6,200 patients with return of spontaneous circulation, only 222 (3.6%) received temperature control therapy and 320 (5.2%) underwent coronary angiography.
  • Initial shockable rhythm in only 2,380 (13.5%); cardiac etiology in 6,569 (37.3%).

Strengths

  • Large and geographically broad: 17,602 patients from 37 hospitals across 29 provinces, which makes this the most substantial in-hospital cardiac arrest dataset from a middle-income country and fills a real gap in global benchmarking.
  • Prospective registry design with standardized outcome definitions (Cerebral Performance Category), rather than retrospective chart abstraction.
  • Follow-up extends to 12 months with neurological status, not just survival to discharge, so the durability of recovery is visible.
  • Process-of-care measures are reported alongside outcomes, which is what makes the dataset actionable: it localises the deficit to post-resuscitation care rather than to the initial response.
  • Reports both return of spontaneous circulation and survival, exposing the attrition between the two (35.2% to 9.1%) that is the key target for improvement.
  • Descriptive aims are matched by descriptive methods, with no overreaching causal claims.

Limitations

  • Purely descriptive with no comparator and no adjusted analysis, so the drivers of the low survival cannot be disentangled; attribution to post-resuscitation care gaps is plausible but not demonstrated.
  • Participating hospitals volunteered for a registry and are not necessarily representative of Chinese hospitals generally, and the direction of that bias is unclear (registry participants may be better resourced than average).
  • The enrolment window (July 2019 to December 2020) overlaps the COVID-19 pandemic, which plausibly affected both arrest incidence and resuscitation practice; no analysis by calendar period is reported.
  • Reported process measures are strikingly favourable (95.9% CPR within 1 minute), which raises concerns about self-reported or retrospectively documented timings in a registry without independent audit.
  • The low proportion with shockable rhythm (13.5%) and cardiac etiology (37.3%) suggests many arrests occurred in patients with advanced non-cardiac illness, so case mix rather than care quality may explain much of the low survival; the infrequent use of do-not-attempt-resuscitation orders, which the authors raise, means resuscitation was attempted in patients for whom it was futile.
  • Six-month survival was prespecified as a secondary outcome but is not reported.
  • No denominators or definitions are given for 'timely' defibrillation or epinephrine, and no measures of variation between the 37 hospitals are reported, which would be the most useful signal for improvement.
  • Data are from 2019 to 2020 and may not reflect current practice; funding source and the completeness of 12-month follow-up are not reported.

Practice implications

For cardiologists this is a benchmarking and systems paper rather than a clinical-decision one. Survival after in-hospital cardiac arrest in this Chinese cohort was about 9%, well below contemporary high-income country figures, and the registry locates the problem after the arrest rather than during it: initial response was fast, but only 3.6% of patients with return of spontaneous circulation received temperature control and 5.2% had coronary angiography. The lesson transferable to any system is to audit the post-resuscitation bundle (critical care access, targeted temperature management, early angiography in suitable patients) rather than only CPR response times. It also underscores the value of advance care planning: where resuscitation is attempted in patients with advanced non-cardiac illness and no do-not-attempt order, measured survival will remain low regardless of technical quality. Case mix means these figures should not be read as a direct indictment of resuscitation quality.

Prognostic implications of complications during VA-ECMO for cardiogenic shock.

Rali AS et al. · PubMed

In 22,547 adults supported with VA-ECMO for cardiogenic shock in the ELSO Registry (2017-2024), in-hospital mortality was 54% and complications were strongly and cumulatively associated with death: neurologic complications tripled the hazard of mortality (HR, 3.6; 95% CI, 3.4-3.8) and raised absolute 30-day mortality risk from 48.6% with no complications to 93.1%.

Design
Retrospective observational cohort study of a multinational voluntary registry (Extracorporeal Life Support Organization Registry), 2017-2024. Three complementary analytic approaches were used: Cox proportional hazards with complications as time-dependent covariates, a multi-state model treating complications as transient states, and landmark Kaplan-Meier analysis of conditional survival given survival to 3 weeks. Covariates included in adjustment are not reported.
Population
22,547 adults with a diagnosis of cardiogenic shock treated with VA-ECMO; 31.7% female. Age, shock etiology, SCAI stage, cannulation strategy, concomitant left ventricular unloading and other baseline characteristics are not reported in the abstract.
Intervention
The exposure is the occurrence, type, timing and number of on-support complications during VA-ECMO (neurologic, pulmonary and other subtypes; individual subtype definitions not reported).
Comparison
Patients on VA-ECMO without the complication in question, with complication status handled as a time-dependent exposure rather than a baseline group.
Outcomes
Primary outcome, in-hospital mortality during the index hospitalization: 12,094 of 22,547 patients (54%). Complications occurred in 74% of non-survivors versus 54% of survivors, with all subtypes more frequent among non-survivors. Neurologic complications increased the hazard of mortality with HR 3.6 (95% CI, 3.4-3.8); the P value is not reported. In the multi-state model, absolute 30-day mortality risk rose from 48.6% with no complications to 93.1% with a neurologic complication (confidence intervals not reported). Landmark analysis showed the highest conditional survival among patients without complications within the first 3 weeks (figures not reported). Hazard ratios for pulmonary and other complication subtypes are not reported numerically.

Other results

  • In-hospital mortality: 12,094 of 22,547 (54%).
  • Complications were more frequent in non-survivors than survivors: 74% vs 54%, with all subtypes occurring more often in non-survivors.
  • Neurologic complications: hazard of mortality increased 3.6-fold (95% CI, 3.4-3.8).
  • Multi-state model absolute 30-day mortality risk: 48.6% with no complications rising to 93.1% with a neurologic complication.
  • Mortality risk rose cumulatively with the number of complications.
  • Landmark analysis: patients free of complications in the first 3 weeks had the highest conditional survival.
  • Neurologic and pulmonary complications carried the greatest mortality risk (pulmonary estimate not reported).

Strengths

  • Very large multinational cohort (22,547 patients) over a contemporary period (2017-2024), giving precision that no single centre could achieve for a therapy this uncommon.
  • Complications were modelled as time-dependent rather than baseline covariates, which avoids the immortal-time bias that invalidates most naive analyses of on-support events.
  • Three analytic approaches (time-dependent Cox, multi-state model, landmark conditional survival) were applied in parallel and gave concordant results, which is a meaningful internal consistency check.
  • The multi-state model reports absolute 30-day risks, not just hazard ratios, which is far more useful at the bedside and for family discussions.
  • Landmark analysis conditional on survival to 3 weeks addresses the question clinicians actually ask about patients still on support, rather than averaging over the whole cohort.
  • Distinguishes complication type and number rather than treating complications as a single binary exposure, identifying neurologic and pulmonary events as the dominant drivers.

Limitations

  • Observational and associative: a complication such as neurologic injury is both a cause of death and a marker of a sicker patient and of the very physiology that led to arrest or cannulation, so the 3.6-fold hazard cannot be read as the preventable fraction.
  • Reverse causation and competing treatment decisions are central here, since a devastating neurologic complication frequently triggers withdrawal of support; a 93.1% 30-day mortality partly reflects that decision rather than the biology alone.
  • Voluntary registry data with site-determined reporting: complication ascertainment, including whether neurologic injury was systematically screened for (imaging, neuromonitoring) versus clinically apparent, is not standardized or reported.
  • The adjustment set is not reported, so residual confounding by severity (SCAI stage, lactate, pre-cannulation arrest, cannulation mode, left ventricular unloading, centre volume) cannot be assessed.
  • Baseline characteristics and shock etiology are not reported, limiting any judgement about to whom these figures apply.
  • Only in-hospital mortality is reported; no neurological function, quality of life, or post-discharge survival outcomes are given, which matters most for a therapy whose purpose is meaningful survival.
  • Estimates for pulmonary and other complication subtypes are asserted but not quantified, and no P values or confidence intervals accompany the multi-state model risks.
  • Registry contributors are self-selected ECMO centres, so case mix and practice are not representative of all centres offering VA-ECMO; funding is not reported.

Practice implications

These data quantify what shock teams observe: once a complication occurs on VA-ECMO, particularly a neurologic one, the prognosis changes decisively, and the risk accumulates with each additional complication. Practically that argues for aggressive complication prevention as the main modifiable lever during support (meticulous cannulation and distal perfusion, attention to differential hypoxia and left ventricular distension, anticoagulation protocols, and early neurologic surveillance so injury is detected rather than inferred late), and for complication status to be an explicit input to daily decisions about escalation, bridging to durable support, or transition to comfort care. The numbers are also useful for family conversations, since absolute 30-day mortality of roughly 49% with no complications versus 93% after a neurologic complication is more informative than a generic survival figure. Because the design is observational and withdrawal of support is entangled with the outcome, these figures should inform prognostication, not be treated as the benefit available from preventing the complication.

Ieki H et al. · PubMed

Abstract

BACKGROUND: Accurate assessment of aortic stenosis (AS) requires integration of both structural and functional information characterized by visual traits as well as quantitation of gradients. OBJECTIVES: The authors aimed to develop and validate an automated multiview deep learning framework to assess AS severity. Its strong performance generalizes robustly to external validation cohorts and shows potential as an automated clinical decision support tool.
