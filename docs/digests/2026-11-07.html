Antithrombotic Therapy After Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion: The SIMPLAAFY Randomized Clinical Trial.

Reddy VY et al. · PubMed

In 1857 patients after successful left atrial appendage closure, both a reduced-dose NOAC for 3 months and aspirin alone were noninferior to 6 months of DAPT for the 6-month composite of death, stroke, systemic embolism, or major bleeding, with less bleeding on either monotherapy.

Design
Open-label, 3-group, multicenter randomized noninferiority trial at 87 US investigational sites, with 1:1:1 allocation; randomization October 2024 to December 2025 and final 6-month follow-up in July 2026. Noninferiority was tested in the primary analysis population with a 1-sided Farrington-Manning test (alpha = .025).
Population
1857 patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation who had undergone successful left atrial appendage closure and were eligible for all 3 regimens. Mean age 76 (SD, 8) years, 42.9% female, mean CHA2DS2-VASc score 4.2 (SD, 1.3), and 68.0% with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.
Intervention
Reduced-dose non-vitamin K oral anticoagulant (NOAC) for 3 months followed by aspirin (n = 620), or aspirin alone for 12 months (n = 613).
Comparison
Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) for 6 months followed by aspirin (n = 624).
Outcomes
Primary outcome was the 6-month composite of all-cause death, stroke, systemic embolism, or major bleeding: 41 patients (6.9%) with DAPT vs 31 (5.1%) with reduced-dose NOAC (risk ratio, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.48-1.18]; risk difference, -1.7 percentage points; 97.5% upper bound, 1.2) and 20 (3.3%) with aspirin alone (risk ratio, 0.49 [95% CI, 0.29-0.82]; risk difference, -3.5 percentage points; 97.5% upper bound, -0.8). Both monotherapy strategies met noninferiority criteria (P < .001 for noninferiority for both). The noninferiority margin was not reported.

Other results

  • Primary composite at 6 months: 6.9% (41/624) with DAPT, 5.1% (31/620) with reduced-dose NOAC, 3.3% (20/613) with aspirin alone.
  • Reduced-dose NOAC vs DAPT: risk ratio 0.75 (95% CI, 0.48-1.18); risk difference -1.7 percentage points, 97.5% upper bound 1.2; noninferiority met (P < .001).
  • Aspirin alone vs DAPT: risk ratio 0.49 (95% CI, 0.29-0.82); risk difference -3.5 percentage points, 97.5% upper bound -0.8; noninferiority met (P < .001).
  • Major bleeding (exploratory, no multiplicity adjustment): 4.5% with DAPT, 2.7% with reduced-dose NOAC, 1.7% with aspirin alone.
  • Clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (exploratory): 8.7% with DAPT, 4.8% with reduced-dose NOAC, 3.8% with aspirin alone.
  • Device-related thrombus at 45 days (exploratory): 3.4% with DAPT, 0.9% with reduced-dose NOAC, 2.7% with aspirin alone.

Strengths

  • Randomized three-group comparison that directly addresses a common post-procedural question for which no adequately powered randomized data previously existed.
  • Large sample (1857 patients) across 87 US sites in a typical older, high-stroke-risk LAAC population (mean CHA2DS2-VASc 4.2), supporting generalizability to US practice.
  • Clinically relevant composite primary endpoint combining thrombotic and bleeding harms, so a regimen cannot look good simply by trading one for the other.
  • Prespecified noninferiority testing with a 1-sided Farrington-Manning test at alpha = .025, and the aspirin-alone arm met noninferiority with a risk difference upper bound below zero.

Limitations

  • Open-label design, so management and ascertainment of bleeding events could be influenced by knowledge of treatment assignment; whether endpoint adjudication was blinded is not reported.
  • Follow-up for the primary endpoint was only 6 months, while the aspirin-alone arm continued to 12 months; longer-term stroke and device-related thrombus outcomes are not reported.
  • Bleeding and 45-day device-related thrombus rates were exploratory and not adjusted for multiplicity, so the apparent bleeding advantage of monotherapy is hypothesis-generating.
  • Enrollment was restricted to patients with uncomplicated, successful closure who were eligible for all 3 regimens, so results do not apply to complicated implants or patients with a specific indication for anticoagulation.
  • Device-related thrombus was numerically highest with DAPT (3.4%) and lowest with reduced-dose NOAC (0.9%) but the comparison was exploratory and event numbers were not reported.
  • The specific NOACs, doses used, and trial funding source are not reported in the abstract.

Practice implications

After uncomplicated left atrial appendage closure in patients eligible for any regimen, 6 months of DAPT no longer looks necessary: aspirin alone, or a reduced-dose NOAC for 3 months followed by aspirin, gave noninferior 6-month composite outcomes with numerically less major and nonmajor bleeding. Aspirin alone is the simplest option and had the lowest primary event rate, though its 2.7% device-related thrombus rate versus 0.9% with reduced-dose NOAC is worth weighing in patients judged at higher thrombotic risk, and these exploratory signals plus the 6-month horizon argue for individualized choice pending longer follow-up.

TAVR in Patients with Moderate Aortic Stenosis with At-Risk Features.

Généreux P et al. · PubMed

In 744 patients with moderate aortic stenosis and at least one at-risk feature, transfemoral TAVR reduced the 2-year composite of death or a heart-failure event from 23.9% to 16.7% (risk difference, -7.2 percentage points; 95% CI, -13.1 to -1.3; P = 0.02), driven entirely by fewer heart-failure events with no difference in mortality.

Design
International, open-label, randomized trial with 1:1 allocation to transfemoral TAVR with a balloon-expandable valve or clinical surveillance. Superiority testing in the intention-to-treat population; funded by Edwards Lifesciences (PROGRESS, NCT04889872).
Population
744 patients with moderate aortic stenosis and at least one at-risk feature (a symptom or a structural or functional abnormality associated with faster disease progression or adverse cardiovascular outcomes). Mean age 78 years, 39.1% women, 45.6% at low surgical risk, mean aortic-valve area 1.2 cm2, mean aortic-valve peak velocity 3.3 m per second.
Intervention
Transfemoral TAVR with a balloon-expandable valve (373 patients).
Comparison
Clinical surveillance, the guideline-recommended strategy of echocardiographic assessment every 1 or 2 years (371 patients).
Outcomes
Primary effectiveness end point was the composite of death from any cause or a heart-failure event with or without hospitalization at 2 years: Kaplan-Meier estimates 16.7% with TAVR vs 23.9% with clinical surveillance (risk difference, -7.2 percentage points; 95% CI, -13.1 to -1.3; P = 0.02 for superiority).

Other results

  • Primary composite of death or heart-failure event at 2 years: 16.7% with TAVR vs 23.9% with clinical surveillance (risk difference, -7.2 percentage points; 95% CI, -13.1 to -1.3; P = 0.02 for superiority).
  • All-cause death at 2 years: 8.4% with TAVR vs 7.3% with clinical surveillance (no effect estimate, CI or P value reported).
  • Heart-failure event at 2 years: 10.8% with TAVR vs 19.0% with clinical surveillance (no effect estimate, CI or P value reported), so the composite benefit was driven by heart-failure events.
  • The incidence of procedure-related adverse events did not differ meaningfully between TAVR patients and surveillance patients who went on to undergo aortic-valve replacement; specific rates were not reported.
  • Crossover rate from surveillance to aortic-valve replacement: not reported.

Strengths

  • Randomized trial addressing a question guidelines currently answer with watchful waiting, in a population (moderate aortic stenosis) far larger than the severe-stenosis population already treated.
  • Intention-to-treat superiority analysis with a clinically meaningful composite of death and heart-failure events rather than a haemodynamic or imaging surrogate.
  • Enrichment for at-risk features targets the patients most likely to progress, which both raises event rates and defines a clinically identifiable group for application of the result.
  • Procedure-related adverse events were compared against surveillance patients who crossed over to valve replacement, a fair comparison of procedural risk.
  • Nearly half the cohort (45.6%) was at low surgical risk, and the mean age of 78 years reflects contemporary TAVR practice.

Limitations

  • Open-label design with no sham procedure, so heart-failure events, which are partly judgement-based and symptom-driven, could be ascertained or adjudicated differently between groups; whether adjudication was blinded is not reported.
  • The composite benefit came entirely from heart-failure events (10.8% vs 19.0%); all-cause death was numerically slightly higher with TAVR (8.4% vs 7.3%), so this is not a mortality benefit.
  • Two-year follow-up is short relative to the lifetime consequences of implanting a valve in patients who do not yet have severe stenosis, including valve durability, reintervention, pacemaker need and coronary access.
  • Funded by the valve manufacturer (Edwards Lifesciences), and results apply only to transfemoral balloon-expandable TAVR.
  • Rates of crossover from surveillance to valve replacement are not reported, so the true contrast between strategies at 2 years is unclear, and the comparator was surveillance rather than early surgery.
  • Confidence interval for the primary risk difference extends to -1.3 percentage points, so the benefit could be considerably smaller than the point estimate; the at-risk-feature definition is broad and the contribution of individual features is not reported.

Practice implications

This is the first randomized evidence that intervening before aortic stenosis becomes severe can change outcomes, and it should prompt a different conversation with patients who have moderate stenosis plus symptoms or structural or functional abnormalities. The benefit is a reduction in heart-failure events, not survival, over 2 years, so the decision turns on symptom burden and heart-failure risk weighed against committing a patient to a prosthetic valve earlier in life. Guideline change is plausible but will likely await longer-term durability and mortality data; in the meantime, careful at-risk-feature phenotyping rather than blanket treatment of moderate stenosis is the reasonable approach.

N Engl J Med 2026-11-01 RCT Interventional n = 1,105 impact 4/5

Coronary CT Angiography versus Intravascular Ultrasound Guidance for PCI.

Collet C et al. · PubMed

In 1105 patients with CCTA-derived FFR of 0.80 or less and a stenosis greater than 70%, a coronary CT angiography-guided PCI strategy was noninferior to IVUS-guided PCI for 12-month target-vessel failure (4.1% vs 4.6%; risk difference, -0.42 percentage points; 95% CI, -2.82 to 1.99; P<0.001 for noninferiority) but not superior (P = 0.73).

Design
Investigator-initiated, international, open-label, randomized noninferiority trial with blinded end-point adjudication and 1:1 allocation. Noninferiority margin for the absolute risk difference was 4.5 percentage points, with a prespecified hierarchical testing strategy for superiority. Funded by GE HealthCare and Heartflow (P4, NCT05253677).
Population
1105 patients with a clinical indication for revascularization, at least one epicardial lesion with a diameter stenosis of more than 70%, and a fractional flow reserve derived from CCTA of 0.80 or less. Median age 65 years, 21.7% women.
Intervention
CCTA-guided PCI strategy, using the anatomical and physiological information available before catheterization for procedural planning (556 patients).
Comparison
IVUS-guided PCI strategy (549 patients).
Outcomes
Primary end point was target-vessel failure, a composite of death from cardiac causes, target-vessel myocardial infarction, or ischemia-driven target-vessel revascularization, at 12 months: 23 patients (4.1%) in the CCTA-guided group vs 25 (4.6%) in the IVUS-guided group (risk difference, -0.42 percentage points; 95% CI, -2.82 to 1.99; P<0.001 for noninferiority; P = 0.73 for superiority).

Other results

  • Target-vessel failure at 12 months: 23/556 (4.1%) with CCTA guidance vs 25/549 (4.6%) with IVUS guidance; risk difference -0.42 percentage points (95% CI, -2.82 to 1.99).
  • Noninferiority met against a prespecified 4.5 percentage point margin (P<0.001); superiority not met (P = 0.73).
  • Clinical procedural complications: 6 patients in the CCTA-guided group and 9 in the IVUS-guided group (no effect estimate, CI or P value reported).
  • Individual components of the composite end point were not reported separately in the abstract.
  • Procedural efficiency measures such as contrast volume, radiation dose and procedure time were not reported.

Strengths

  • Randomized comparison against IVUS guidance, the strategy already shown to improve outcomes, rather than against angiography alone, so the control arm represents best current practice.
  • Blinded end-point adjudication, which matters because the composite includes ischemia-driven revascularization, a decision susceptible to operator knowledge of imaging strategy.
  • Investigator-initiated with a prespecified noninferiority margin and a hierarchical testing strategy for superiority, limiting post hoc interpretation.
  • Enrollment required both an anatomical criterion (>70% stenosis) and a physiological one (CCTA-derived FFR <=0.80), giving a well characterized population in which CCTA planning is actually feasible.
  • The 95% CI for the risk difference (-2.82 to 1.99) is narrow and well inside the 4.5 percentage point margin, so the noninferiority conclusion is not borderline.

Limitations

  • Open-label strategy trial, so although adjudication was blinded, operators knew the assigned imaging strategy and this could influence revascularization decisions during follow-up.
  • Twelve-month follow-up is short for stent-related outcomes; the benefit of intravascular imaging on target-vessel failure typically accrues over longer periods.
  • Only 48 primary events occurred in total, so the trial has limited power to detect differences in individual components or in safety signals, and the low event rate reflects a relatively selected population.
  • Funded by the CCTA technology manufacturers (GE HealthCare and Heartflow), the parties with a commercial interest in the experimental strategy.
  • Results apply only to patients whose anatomy and physiology were confirmed suitable on CCTA beforehand, excluding those with heavy calcification, prior stents, poor image quality or arrhythmia who make up much of real-world PCI practice.
  • Noninferiority, not superiority, was demonstrated, and important practical outcomes (contrast load, radiation, procedure duration, cost, and whether CCTA replaced or added to invasive imaging) are not reported.
  • Only 21.7% of participants were women, limiting inference by sex.

Practice implications

Where high-quality CCTA with derived FFR is already obtained before catheterization, using it to plan PCI yields 12-month target-vessel failure rates indistinguishable from mandatory IVUS, which offers a route to pre-planned, standardized procedures without an intravascular imaging run. This does not displace intravascular imaging for the complex or calcified lesions that fall outside the trial's entry criteria, and because it is a noninferiority result from a manufacturer-funded trial with only 48 events at 1 year, IVUS remains the default where CCTA infrastructure, image quality or operator experience is lacking.

N Engl J Med 2026-11-05 RCT Prevention and lipids n = 757 impact 4/5

Plozasiran for Severe Hypertriglyceridemia and Risk of Pancreatitis.

Watts GF et al. · PubMed

In two identically designed placebo-controlled trials of 757 adults with severe hypertriglyceridemia, quarterly plozasiran 25 mg lowered median triglycerides by 79% and 81% at month 12 (placebo-adjusted -45 and -47 percentage points) and, in a pooled analysis, reduced adjudicated acute pancreatitis from 5 to 1 per 100 patient-years (rate ratio, 0.22; P = 0.008).

Design
Two double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trials (SHASTA-3, NCT06347003; SHASTA-4, NCT06347016) with identical designs, end points and analytic plans, 2:1 allocation, and dosing at baseline and months 3, 6 and 9. Acute pancreatitis was addressed in a pooled analysis of adjudicated events. Funded by Arrowhead Pharmaceuticals.
Population
757 adults with severe hypertriglyceridemia across the two trials. Baseline characteristics beyond the presence of severe hypertriglyceridemia, including age, sex and baseline triglyceride level, are not reported; a prespecified secondary analysis covered patients with a baseline triglyceride level of 880 mg per deciliter or greater.
Intervention
Plozasiran 25 mg, a small interfering RNA targeting APOC3 messenger RNA, given at baseline and at months 3, 6 and 9.
Comparison
Placebo on the same schedule.
Outcomes
Primary end point was the percent change in fasting plasma triglyceride level from baseline to month 12: median reductions of 79% and 81% from baseline in the two plozasiran groups, with placebo-adjusted median differences of -45 percentage points (95% CI, -51 to -38) and -47 percentage points (95% CI, -54 to -39). Pooled adjudicated acute pancreatitis: 1 per 100 patient-years with plozasiran vs 5 per 100 patient-years with placebo (rate ratio, 0.22; P = 0.008); 95% CI for the rate ratio not reported.

Other results

  • Median triglyceride reduction from baseline at month 12: 79% and 81% with plozasiran in the two trials.
  • Placebo-adjusted median change at month 12: -45 percentage points (95% CI, -51 to -38) and -47 percentage points (95% CI, -54 to -39).
  • Triglyceride level below 500 mg per deciliter (5.6 mmol/L) at 12 months: 91% and 93% with plozasiran vs 51% and 50% with placebo (P<0.001).
  • Pooled adjudicated acute pancreatitis: 1 per 100 patient-years with plozasiran vs 5 per 100 patient-years with placebo (rate ratio, 0.22; P = 0.008).
  • Adverse events during treatment: 73% in both the pooled plozasiran and placebo groups.
  • Results for the other key secondary end points (month 10 triglyceride change, triglycerides below 150 mg per deciliter, the subgroup with baseline triglycerides >=880 mg per deciliter, remnant cholesterol and non-HDL cholesterol) are not reported in the abstract.

Strengths

  • Two independent double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trials with identical designs, end points and analytic plans, so the triglyceride result is internally replicated (-45 and -47 percentage points placebo-adjusted).
  • The pancreatitis analysis used adjudicated events rather than investigator-reported diagnoses, and pancreatitis is the clinical harm that actually motivates treating severe hypertriglyceridemia.
  • Large effect on the biomarker with a high proportion reaching the clinically relevant threshold of triglycerides below 500 mg per deciliter (91% and 93% vs 51% and 50%, P<0.001).
  • Quarterly subcutaneous dosing in a condition where adherence to existing therapy is a real problem.
  • Overall treatment-emergent adverse event rates were identical to placebo (73% in both), with no excess signal apparent at 12 months.

Limitations

  • The primary end point was a biomarker (percent change in triglycerides), not a clinical outcome; the pancreatitis finding came from a pooled analysis across both trials rather than from a prespecified primary clinical end point in either.
  • Pancreatitis event numbers are not reported, only rates per 100 patient-years, and no confidence interval is given for the rate ratio of 0.22, so the precision of the clinical benefit cannot be assessed from the abstract.
  • Whether the pooled pancreatitis analysis was prespecified with multiplicity control is not clear, and the placebo rate of 5 per 100 patient-years implies a small absolute number of events over 12 months.
  • Funded by the manufacturer (Arrowhead Pharmaceuticals), with no independent replication outside the sponsor's trial programme.
  • Twelve-month follow-up gives no information on durability, long-term safety of sustained APOC3 knockdown, or effect on atherosclerotic cardiovascular events.
  • Baseline population characteristics, background lipid-lowering therapy and the proportion with prior pancreatitis or familial chylomicronemia are not reported, limiting judgement of generalizability.
  • Notably, half the placebo group also reached triglycerides below 500 mg per deciliter, suggesting substantial regression to the mean or background therapy effects in this population.

Practice implications

Plozasiran gives a large, replicated triglyceride reduction in severe hypertriglyceridemia, and the pooled signal of fewer adjudicated acute pancreatitis events is the first suggestion that an APOC3-targeted agent can prevent the complication that matters most in this group. For the cardiologist or lipidologist managing patients with triglycerides in the severe range and a history or risk of pancreatitis, this positions plozasiran as a likely future option alongside olezarsen, but the pancreatitis benefit rests on a pooled analysis with unreported event counts and no confidence interval, so treatment decisions should await the full publication and regulatory review rather than being changed on this result alone.

Hydralazine-isosorbide dinitrate in chronic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction: the H-HeFT trial.

Køber L et al. · PubMed

In 592 patients with HFrEF on contemporary therapy at 23 Danish hospitals, hydralazine-isosorbide dinitrate did not reduce the composite of worsening heart failure events or death over a mean 3.7 years (30.6% vs 30.5%; HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.67-1.21; P = .48), a clearly negative result for a legacy therapy.

Design
Multicentre, randomized (1:1), double-blind, placebo-controlled trial at 23 Danish hospitals (H-HeFT). Mean follow-up 3.7 years. Secondary endpoints included components of the primary outcome.
Population
592 predominantly White patients with chronic HFrEF: New York Heart Association class II-IV, elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, and LVEF <=40%. Median age 72 years, 18% women.
Intervention
Hydralazine-isosorbide dinitrate, target dose hydralazine 75 mg plus isosorbide dinitrate 40 mg three times daily (294 patients).
Comparison
Matching placebo (298 patients), on top of contemporary heart failure treatment.
Outcomes
Primary endpoint was a composite of hospitalization with worsening heart failure, an urgent outpatient visit resulting in intravenous or metolazone therapy for heart failure, heart transplantation, left ventricular assist device implantation, or death: 90 of 294 patients (30.6%) with H-ISDN vs 91 of 298 (30.5%) with placebo (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.67-1.21; P = .48).

Other results

  • Primary composite: 90/294 (30.6%) with H-ISDN vs 91/298 (30.5%) with placebo; HR 0.90 (95% CI 0.67-1.21); P = .48.
  • Deaths: 57 in the H-ISDN group vs 72 with placebo; HR 0.72 (95% CI 0.51-1.02), a nominally favourable but non-significant secondary finding.
  • Worsening heart failure events: 52 vs 47 patients; HR 1.01 (95% CI 0.68-1.50), with no signal of benefit.
  • Rates of achieving the target dose of hydralazine 75 mg/isosorbide dinitrate 40 mg three times daily, and discontinuation or adverse event rates, are not reported.
  • Background use of contemporary therapy (renin-angiotensin system inhibitors or sacubitril-valsartan, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors) is not quantified in the abstract.
  • Funding source: not reported.

Strengths

  • Double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized design directly retesting a legacy therapy in the era of contemporary quadruple HFrEF treatment, which is exactly the question clinicians need answered.
  • Long mean follow-up of 3.7 years with a hard, clinically meaningful composite that captures urgent outpatient intravenous or metolazone treatment as well as hospitalization, transplantation, LVAD and death.
  • Enrolment required objective disease severity (LVEF <=40% plus elevated NT-proBNP), so the population was genuinely at risk, and the 30% event rate confirms this.
  • Multicentre across 23 hospitals with a pragmatic, nationwide Danish setting and near-complete event capture typical of Danish registries.
  • The point estimate and confidence interval for the primary endpoint (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.67-1.21) sit close to unity with event rates essentially identical, so this is an informative negative result rather than an uninterpretable one.

Limitations

  • 592 patients and 181 primary events give limited power; the 95% CI (0.67-1.21) does not exclude a 33% relative risk reduction, so a moderate benefit cannot be formally ruled out.
  • The mortality signal (HR 0.72; 95% CI 0.51-1.02) is a secondary endpoint, not multiplicity-controlled, and borders significance, which invites over-interpretation given the flat primary result.
  • Predominantly White Danish population with only 18% women, so the findings cannot be extrapolated to self-identified Black patients, the group in whom the original A-HeFT benefit was demonstrated and in whom current guideline recommendations apply.
  • Achieved doses, tolerability and discontinuation rates are not reported, so it is unclear whether a null result reflects lack of efficacy or inability to maintain the thrice-daily target dose.
  • Background contemporary therapy is asserted but not quantified, so the degree to which patients were truly optimized on quadruple therapy is unknown.
  • Funding source is not reported.

Practice implications

Adding hydralazine-isosorbide dinitrate to contemporary HFrEF therapy did not reduce heart failure events or death in this predominantly White Danish cohort, which argues against using it as a routine add-on in such patients and supports directing effort instead at optimizing the four guideline-directed drug classes. The trial does not overturn the guideline recommendation in self-identified Black patients, who were not represented here, and the non-significant mortality difference is a secondary observation that should not drive prescribing. Practically, this is a useful de-prescribing signal for a burdensome thrice-daily regimen in patients who do not fit the A-HeFT population.

Circulation 2026-10-31 RCT Structural and valve n = 210 impact 4/5

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in Pure Aortic Regurgitation: The Randomized SEASON-AR Clinical Trial.

Gao XF et al. · PubMed

In 210 patients with severe pure aortic regurgitation unsuitable for or declining surgery, transfemoral TAVR with the VitaFlow system plus medical therapy cut the 1-year composite of death, disabling stroke or heart failure rehospitalization from 24.8% to 8.3% (HR 0.31; 95% CI, 0.14-0.66; P=0.001), driven by fewer heart failure rehospitalizations, at the cost of more early bleeding and pacemaker implantation.

Design
Prospective, investigator-initiated, multicenter, randomized, open-label trial at 18 centers in China with 1:1 allocation (SEASON-AR, NCT04864145).
Population
210 patients with severe pure aortic regurgitation for whom surgery was considered difficult or unacceptable by the local heart team, or who persistently declined surgery. Age, sex, surgical risk scores, left ventricular size and function, and other baseline characteristics are not reported in the abstract.
Intervention
Transfemoral TAVR with the VitaFlow system plus guideline-directed medical therapy (109 patients).
Comparison
Guideline-directed medical therapy alone (101 patients).
Outcomes
Primary end point was the composite of all-cause death, disabling stroke, or heart failure rehospitalization at 1 year: Kaplan-Meier estimates 8.3% with TAVR vs 24.8% with GDMT (HR, 0.31 [95% CI, 0.14-0.66]; P=0.001).

Other results

  • Primary composite at 1 year: 8.3% with TAVR plus GDMT vs 24.8% with GDMT alone; HR 0.31 (95% CI, 0.14-0.66); P=0.001.
  • Heart failure rehospitalization: 3.7% vs 21.3%; HR 0.16 (95% CI, 0.06-0.47); P<0.001, the main driver of the composite.
  • All-cause death and disabling stroke were not reported separately in the abstract.
  • TAVR was associated with higher rates of major or life-threatening bleeding and new permanent pacemaker implantation, most occurring within 30 days of the procedure; rates, effect estimates and P values for these harms are not reported.
  • Crossover from GDMT to valve replacement is not reported.
  • Funding source is not reported.

Strengths

  • First adequately powered randomized comparison of TAVR against medical therapy alone in severe pure aortic regurgitation, a population with no device-based evidence base and no dedicated approved device in most regions.
  • The effect size is large and the confidence interval excludes no effect by a wide margin (HR 0.31; 95% CI, 0.14-0.66), so the modest sample size of 210 is sufficient for the question as asked.
  • Investigator-initiated and multicenter across 18 sites, reducing the influence of a single expert operator.
  • Clinically meaningful composite of death, disabling stroke and heart failure rehospitalization rather than haemodynamic or echocardiographic surrogates.
  • Procedural harms (bleeding, new pacemaker) are reported alongside benefit rather than omitted, and their early timing is specified.

Limitations

  • Open-label with no sham, and the composite was driven almost entirely by heart failure rehospitalization (3.7% vs 21.3%), a soft, judgement-dependent endpoint highly susceptible to unblinded management decisions; whether events were adjudicated by a blinded committee is not reported.
  • Only 210 patients with few hard events; death and disabling stroke are not reported separately, so there is no evidence of a mortality benefit.
  • One-year follow-up gives no information on valve durability, late paravalvular regurgitation or device migration, which are the particular concerns when implanting into a non-calcified annulus.
  • Conducted entirely in China with a single device (VitaFlow), so results may not transfer to other populations or to other transcatheter valves, especially those not designed for aortic regurgitation anatomy.
  • Harm rates for major or life-threatening bleeding and new permanent pacemaker implantation are stated only qualitatively, without numbers, confidence intervals or P values, so the risk-benefit trade-off cannot be quantified.
  • Enrolment depended on local heart team judgement that surgery was difficult or unacceptable, plus patients who declined surgery, which is a subjective and heterogeneous entry criterion limiting reproducibility.
  • Baseline characteristics and funding source are not reported.

Practice implications

For patients with severe pure aortic regurgitation who are turned down for or refuse surgery, this trial provides the first randomized support for transcatheter treatment: a two-thirds relative reduction in a composite driven by far fewer heart failure rehospitalizations, offset by more early bleeding and pacemaker implantation. It strengthens the case for referring such patients to a heart team with transcatheter expertise rather than defaulting to medical therapy, but the benefit rests on an unblinded soft endpoint with a single non-dedicated device in a Chinese population at 1 year, so it should not yet be read as licensing routine TAVR for pure regurgitation in surgical candidates or with devices untested in this anatomy.

Eur J Prev Cardiol 2026-11-02 Meta-analysis Prevention and lipids n = 77,178 impact 4/5

PCSK9-Targeting Therapies and Mortality: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Trials.

Navarese EP et al. · PubMed

Abstract

AIMS: To evaluate the association of PCSK9-targeting therapies with mortality and cardiovascular events in stable or clinically stabilized randomized trial populations. METHODS: We systematically reviewed randomized trials comparing alirocumab, evolocumab or inclisiran with placebo or active control. The review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261395609). Treatment effects were pooled as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) using random-effects models. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Prespecified subgroup analyses were based on trial-level known atherosclerosis status, as defined by enrollment criteria. RESULTS: Thirty-two trials enrolled 77,178 participants. Individual atherosclerosis status could not be determined beyond trial-level eligibility criteria. Mortality estimates were predominantly informed by large cardiovascular outcome trials. All-cause mortality was lower with PCSK9-targeting therapy (IRR 0.88, 95% CI 0.82-0.95). Estimates favored PCSK9-targeting treatment in both trials requiring known atherosclerosis for enrollment (IRR 0.90, 95% CI 0.83-0.98) and trials without such an enrollment requirement (IRR 0.78, 95% CI 0.62-0.97; interaction p = 0.21). PCSK9-targeting treatment additionally reduced myocardial infarction, stroke, and coronary revascularization rates. Leave-one-out estimates remained directionally consistent. CONCLUSION: PCSK9-targeting therapies were associated with lower all-cause mortality and fewer ischemic events across trial populations. The predominant contribution of large cardiovascular outcome trials enrolling populations with known atherosclerosis and using PCSK9 antibodies limits the ability to infer a survival benefit in populations not selected on the basis of atherosclerosis status and across individual therapies, particularly inclisiran.
