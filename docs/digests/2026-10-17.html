Circulation 2026-10-13 RCT secondary analysis Thrombosis and vascular n = 12,254 impact 4/5

Bleeding With Asundexian Versus Placebo in Patients With Noncardioembolic Ischemic Stroke or High-Risk TIA: OCEANIC-STROKE Randomized Trial.

Eikelboom JW et al. · PubMed

In this prespecified bleeding analysis of OCEANIC-STROKE (12,254 participants), adding the factor XIa inhibitor asundexian to antiplatelet therapy after noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA did not significantly increase major bleeding (1.9% vs 1.7%; csHR 1.10 [95% CI, 0.85-1.44]; P=0.46), but lower gastrointestinal bleeding was more frequent (1.7% vs 1.0%; csHR 1.75 [95% CI, 1.27-2.41]).

Design
Prespecified safety/bleeding analysis of a randomized, placebo-controlled trial (OCEANIC-STROKE; NCT05686070), with while-on-treatment analyses including events through 2 days after the last dose and death and permanent discontinuation handled as competing risks.
Population
Safety population of 12,254 participants with noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or high-risk transient ischemic attack who received at least 1 dose of study drug (asundexian n=6,124; placebo n=6,130).
Intervention
Asundexian added to background antiplatelet therapy (dose not reported in the abstract).
Comparison
Placebo added to background antiplatelet therapy.
Outcomes
Bleeding categorized by International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria as major, clinically relevant nonmajor (CRNM), or minor. Major bleeding: 117 (1.9%) with asundexian vs 107 (1.7%) with placebo (csHR 1.10 [95% CI, 0.85-1.44]; P=0.46). Major or CRNM bleeding: 339 (5.5%) vs 307 (5.0%) (csHR 1.12 [95% CI, 0.96-1.30]; P=0.16). Any bleeding: 763 (12.5%) vs 752 (12.3%) (csHR 1.02 [95% CI, 0.93-1.13]; P=0.65).

Other results

  • Major bleeding: 117 (1.9%) asundexian vs 107 (1.7%) placebo; csHR 1.10 (95% CI, 0.85-1.44); P=0.46.
  • Major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding: 339 (5.5%) vs 307 (5.0%); csHR 1.12 (95% CI, 0.96-1.30); P=0.16.
  • Any bleeding: 763 (12.5%) vs 752 (12.3%); csHR 1.02 (95% CI, 0.93-1.13); P=0.65.
  • Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage: 41 (0.7%) with asundexian vs 36 (0.6%) with placebo (comparative effect estimate not reported).
  • Any gastrointestinal bleeding: 186 (3.0%) vs 147 (2.4%); csHR 1.28 (95% CI, 1.03-1.59). Lower gastrointestinal bleeding: 102 (1.7%) vs 59 (1.0%); csHR 1.75 (95% CI, 1.27-2.41), primarily rectal bleeding.
  • Treatment effects on major and major or CRNM bleeding were consistent across prespecified subgroups, including planned dual antiplatelet therapy.
  • Procedure-related bleeding and mucosal bleeding did not differ significantly (effect estimates not reported).

Strengths

  • Large randomized, placebo-controlled safety population (12,254 participants) with balanced group sizes, so bleeding comparisons are not confounded by indication.
  • Bleeding adjudicated using standard ISTH categories, with reporting by severity and by anatomical site rather than a single composite.
  • While-on-treatment analysis with death and permanent discontinuation treated as competing risks, which is the appropriate framework for drug-attributable bleeding.
  • Prespecified subgroup analyses, including the clinically important subgroup receiving planned dual antiplatelet therapy, showed consistency.
  • Directly addresses the key clinical question for factor XIa inhibition: whether the ischemic benefit seen in the parent trial comes at a bleeding cost.

Limitations

  • Secondary safety analysis of a completed trial, so the site-specific and subgroup comparisons are exploratory and not adjusted for multiplicity; the lower gastrointestinal bleeding finding could reflect chance among many comparisons.
  • Absolute event numbers for the more severe endpoints are modest (117 vs 107 major bleeds; 41 vs 36 symptomatic intracranial hemorrhages), so the confidence interval for major bleeding (0.85-1.44) cannot exclude a clinically meaningful 44% relative increase.
  • Intracranial hemorrhage was reported descriptively without a hazard ratio or P value, limiting interpretation of the endpoint cardiologists and neurologists most fear.
  • Asundexian dose, duration of exposure, follow-up time and background antiplatelet regimens are not reported in the abstract.
  • While-on-treatment censoring 2 days after the last dose excludes later events and may understate bleeding in patients who discontinue.
  • Efficacy outcomes are not re-reported here, so net clinical benefit cannot be computed from this analysis alone; funding and sponsor role are not reported in the abstract.

Practice implications

Asundexian is not yet approved, but these data support the central premise of factor XIa inhibition: meaningful antithrombotic effect without the expected excess in major or intracranial bleeding when added to antiplatelet therapy after noncardioembolic stroke. The signal for lower gastrointestinal bleeding, mostly rectal, is the practical caveat and suggests that if the drug reaches clinic, patients with prior gastrointestinal bleeding or anorectal pathology will warrant closer counselling and a low threshold to investigate new rectal bleeding rather than attribute it to haemorrhoids.

Intraoperative Dobutamine Plus Norepinephrine to Prevent Postoperative Acute Kidney Injury in Septic Shock: A Randomized Clinical Trial.

Chinese Perioperative Septic Shock Investigators for the Chinese Peri-Operative Organ Protection Group et al. · PubMed

In 584 adults with septic shock undergoing surgical source control at 39 Chinese hospitals, adding intraoperative dobutamine (up to 5 ug/kg/min) to norepinephrine reduced new-onset or worsening AKI within 7 postoperative days from 34.7% (102/294) to 22.8% (66/290) (adjusted risk ratio 0.64 [95% CI, 0.49-0.83]), with more tachycardia and no difference in 9 of 11 secondary outcomes.

Design
Randomized, open-label clinical trial at 39 tertiary hospitals in China, enrolling June 2024 to August 2025 with follow-up ending 15 November 2025 (NCT06462313).
Population
584 adults with septic shock undergoing surgical source control (median [IQR] age 67.5 [55.0-76.0] years; 192 [32.9%] female); 290 assigned to dobutamine plus norepinephrine and 294 to norepinephrine alone.
Intervention
Intraoperative intravenous dobutamine infusion up to 5 ug/kg/min combined with norepinephrine.
Comparison
Norepinephrine alone.
Outcomes
Primary outcome was new-onset AKI or worsening of preoperative AKI within 7 days after surgery by KDIGO criteria: 22.8% (66/290) with dobutamine plus norepinephrine vs 34.7% (102/294) with norepinephrine alone (adjusted risk ratio 0.64 [95% CI, 0.49-0.83]). There were 11 secondary outcomes, including SOFA score on postoperative day 1 and urine output 1 hour post intervention.

Other results

  • Primary outcome (new-onset or worsening AKI within 7 postoperative days): 22.8% (66/290) vs 34.7% (102/294); adjusted risk ratio 0.64 (95% CI, 0.49-0.83).
  • Stage 2 AKI: 4.8% vs 9.9%; adjusted risk ratio 0.48 (95% CI, 0.26-0.88). Stage 3 AKI: 7.9% vs 13.6%; adjusted risk ratio 0.56 (95% CI, 0.35-0.91).
  • SOFA score on postoperative day 1: adjusted difference -0.53 (95% CI, -0.98 to -0.07), favouring dobutamine plus norepinephrine.
  • Urine output 1 hour after intervention: adjusted difference 0.55 (95% CI, 0.03 to 1.07) mL/kg/h, higher with dobutamine plus norepinephrine.
  • No significant differences in 9 of the 11 secondary outcomes.
  • Tachycardia (heart rate >100/min) occurred more frequently with dobutamine plus norepinephrine (rates and effect estimate not reported).
  • Mortality was not reported among the results in the abstract.

Strengths

  • Randomized design addressing a question previously supported only by very low-certainty evidence despite a guideline recommendation for dobutamine in septic shock with cardiac dysfunction.
  • Multicentre (39 tertiary hospitals), giving reasonable spread of practice within the host country.
  • Clinically meaningful, objectively defined primary outcome using standard KDIGO criteria, with a large absolute reduction (22.8% vs 34.7%).
  • Internally consistent effect across AKI severity, with reductions in both stage 2 (ARR 0.48) and stage 3 (ARR 0.56) injury rather than only mild creatinine shifts.
  • Supportive physiological signals in the same direction (lower day 1 SOFA, higher early urine output).
  • Prospectively registered with a prespecified primary outcome and a defined follow-up end date.

Limitations

  • Open-label with no placebo infusion, so decisions about fluids, vasopressors, nephrotoxins and renal replacement therapy could have been influenced by knowledge of allocation - a major concern for a creatinine- and urine-output-based endpoint.
  • The primary outcome is AKI within 7 days rather than a patient-important outcome; mortality, dialysis dependence and length of stay are not reported in the abstract.
  • Nine of 11 secondary outcomes showed no difference, and the two positive ones (day 1 SOFA, 1-hour urine output) have confidence intervals that approach the null, with no apparent correction for multiplicity.
  • Conducted entirely in Chinese tertiary hospitals; baseline AKI rates of 34.7% in the control arm are high, so absolute benefit may not transfer to settings with different practice.
  • Reported effects are adjusted risk ratios with no unadjusted figures or P values given, and the adjustment model is not described in the abstract.
  • Tachycardia was more frequent but arrhythmia, myocardial injury and other dobutamine-specific harms are not quantified; whether patients had baseline cardiac dysfunction (the guideline indication) is not stated.
  • Follow-up limited to the early postoperative period; funding and sponsor role are not reported.

Practice implications

For cardiologists consulted on septic shock in the perioperative setting, this is the first randomized support for adding low-dose dobutamine to norepinephrine during surgical source control, with a substantial reduction in postoperative AKI including stage 2 and 3 injury. The open-label design and the absence of reported mortality or dialysis outcomes mean this should not yet be treated as a mandate; it is reasonable to consider low-dose dobutamine in septic shock patients with hypoperfusion going to the operating room, while monitoring for tachycardia and awaiting confirmation in blinded trials reporting patient-important endpoints.

Early Discharge Strategy Including Subcutaneous Furosemide in Hospitalized Heart Failure.

Campbell RT et al. · PubMed

In SUBCUT-HF II, an open-label UK trial of 172 patients hospitalized for heart failure and still requiring parenteral diuretics, early discharge on wearable-minipump subcutaneous furosemide gained 4.0 more days alive and out of hospital at 30 days (95% CI: 2.1-5.8; P < 0.001) and shortened index admission by 5.5 days (95% CI: 7.4-3.6; P < 0.001), at the cost of numerically more heart failure readmissions (14% vs 6%; OR 2.45 [95% CI: 0.81-7.43]).

Design
Multicenter, open-label, randomized, active-controlled trial in the United Kingdom (SUBCUT-HF II; NCT05419115).
Population
172 patients hospitalized for heart failure who required ongoing parenteral diuretic therapy; 92 assigned to the early discharge strategy and 80 to usual care.
Intervention
Early discharge strategy with pH-neutral subcutaneous furosemide delivered by a wearable, patient-operated minipump, plus postdischarge review every 3 days while receiving subcutaneous furosemide.
Comparison
Usual inpatient care with continued conventional parenteral diuretic therapy. Both groups had a 2-week clinic review.
Outcomes
Primary outcome was days alive and out of hospital (DAOH) at 30 days after randomization: 4.0 more DAOH with early discharge (95% CI: 2.1-5.8 days; P < 0.001).

Other results

  • Primary outcome, DAOH at 30 days: 4.0 days more with the early discharge strategy (95% CI: 2.1-5.8 days; P < 0.001).
  • Index hospitalization shorter by a mean of 5.5 days (95% CI: 7.4-3.6 days; P < 0.001).
  • DAOH benefit sustained at 60 days: difference 4.2 days (95% CI: 0.9-7.6 days).
  • Heart failure readmissions: 13 (14%) in the early discharge group vs 5 (6%) with usual care; OR 2.45 (95% CI: 0.81-7.43).
  • Serious adverse events: 25% with early discharge vs 23% with usual care.
  • Device-related problems occurred with 3% of minipump placements, none serious.

Strengths

  • Randomized and multicenter, testing a complete care strategy rather than a drug, which is how the intervention would be used in practice.
  • DAOH is a patient-centred primary endpoint that captures both survival and hospital burden, and the benefit was large relative to its confidence interval (4.0 days, 95% CI 2.1-5.8).
  • Benefit persisted at 60 days (4.2 days, 95% CI 0.9-7.6), so the gain was not merely a shifted admission.
  • Enrolled the clinically relevant population - patients still requiring parenteral diuretics - rather than patients already ready for discharge.
  • Structured postdischarge safety net (review every 3 days) that is reproducible, with serious adverse events similar between arms (25% vs 23%) and few device problems (3%, none serious).

Limitations

  • Small trial (172 patients; 92 vs 80) and open-label, with no blinding of patients, clinicians or outcome ascertainment; length of stay and discharge timing are investigator-determined and highly susceptible to performance bias.
  • Unequal group sizes (92 vs 80) are unexplained in the abstract and raise questions about the randomization procedure.
  • DAOH is partly a direct consequence of the intervention itself (earlier discharge), so a positive result is close to inevitable by design; it does not establish clinical benefit.
  • Heart failure readmissions were more than twice as frequent with early discharge (14% vs 6%); the trial was underpowered for this outcome (OR 2.45, 95% CI 0.81-7.43) and cannot exclude meaningful harm.
  • No reported effect on mortality, renal function, electrolytes, symptoms or quality of life in the abstract, and no cost-effectiveness data despite the economic rationale.
  • Single-country (UK) trial with intensive 3-day review, requiring both the pH-neutral subcutaneous furosemide formulation and a community follow-up infrastructure that many systems lack.
  • Follow-up limited to 60 days; funding and sponsor role are not reported.

Practice implications

For selected, otherwise stable patients whose only reason to remain in hospital is ongoing parenteral diuresis, subcutaneous furosemide via a wearable minipump with structured 3-day review can plausibly free several hospital days without an excess of serious adverse events. The trial is small, open-label and shows a doubling of heart failure readmissions that it cannot rule out as real, so this is best viewed as a promising, resource-dependent pathway for carefully chosen patients and systems that can guarantee close follow-up, rather than an established standard.

Immediate Versus Staged Complete Revascularization in Multivessel Disease and STEMI: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Trials.

O'Callaghan D et al. · PubMed

In a meta-analysis of 4 randomized trials (2,651 patients) with STEMI and multivessel disease, immediate complete revascularization was associated with higher 1-year all-cause mortality than a staged strategy (HR 1.50; 95% CI 1.02-2.21; P = 0.04), a statistically fragile signal that argues against routine immediate nonculprit PCI.

Design
Frequentist pairwise random-effects meta-analysis of multicenter randomized controlled trials; protocol registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251124207).
Population
Patients presenting with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and multivessel disease enrolled in multicenter RCTs; 4 trials comprising 2,651 patients. Trials required exclusive use of second-generation or newer drug-eluting stents and follow-up of at least 1 year.
Intervention
Immediate complete revascularization, with nonculprit lesion PCI performed during the index procedure.
Comparison
Staged complete revascularization, with nonculprit lesion PCI performed at a later time.
Outcomes
Primary outcome was all-cause mortality: higher with immediate complete revascularization (HR 1.50; 95% CI 1.02-2.21; P = 0.04; prediction interval 0.80-2.81; I2 = 0%; tau2 = 0). Secondary outcomes were myocardial infarction, cardiac death and unplanned revascularization.

Other results

  • All-cause death at 1 year: HR 1.50 (95% CI 1.02-2.21; P = 0.04; prediction interval 0.80-2.81; I2 = 0%; tau2 = 0) with immediate versus staged complete revascularization.
  • Recurrent MI using protocol-specified definitions that included procedural MI: HR 0.58 (95% CI 0.36-0.94; P = 0.02; prediction interval 0.22-1.54; I2 = 0%; tau2 = 0.034), favouring immediate revascularization.
  • Spontaneous MI: HR 0.91 (95% CI 0.55-1.48; P = 0.90; prediction interval 0.41-2.00; I2 = 0%; tau2 = 0.00), no difference.
  • Cardiac death and unplanned revascularization were comparable between strategies (effect estimates not reported in the abstract).
  • Statistical heterogeneity was absent for the mortality endpoint (I2 = 0%), but the prediction interval crossed 1.0 (0.80-2.81).

Strengths

  • Restricted to multicenter randomized trials, so the comparison of timing is based on randomized evidence rather than registry data.
  • Prespecified inclusion criteria requiring contemporary second-generation or newer drug-eluting stents and at least 1 year of follow-up, improving applicability to current practice.
  • Protocol registered prospectively on PROSPERO and analysed with random-effects models reporting prediction intervals as well as confidence intervals.
  • No statistical heterogeneity for the mortality and MI endpoints (I2 = 0%), suggesting consistency of direction across the 4 trials.
  • Directly addresses a decision clinicians face in every STEMI patient with multivessel disease.

Limitations

  • Only 4 trials and 2,651 patients, with a relatively low number of deaths; the authors themselves describe the mortality finding as statistically fragile.
  • The mortality confidence interval barely excludes 1.0 (1.02-2.21) and the prediction interval (0.80-2.81) includes no effect, so a future trial could show no harm.
  • All-cause mortality was not the primary endpoint of the contributing trials, raising the possibility of a chance finding; cardiac death showed no difference, which is biologically discordant with an all-cause mortality excess.
  • Trial-level (not patient-level) HRs were pooled, preventing adjustment for baseline differences or analysis of subgroups such as shock, lesion complexity or timing of the staged procedure.
  • The apparent MI benefit of immediate revascularization depended on definitions including procedural MI and disappeared for spontaneous MI, so the endpoint advantage may be largely definitional.
  • Staging intervals, nonculprit assessment methods and crossover rates across the included trials are not reported in the abstract, and funding was not reported.

Practice implications

These pooled randomized data do not support routinely completing nonculprit PCI during the index STEMI procedure. For haemodynamically stable patients, deferring nonculprit revascularization to a staged procedure remains the safer default, while accepting that the mortality signal rests on few events and that the apparent reduction in recurrent MI with immediate treatment is driven by periprocedural events. Immediate treatment may still be reasonable for selected patients with ongoing ischaemia or instability, where the trials provide little guidance.

Eur J Prev Cardiol 2026-10-12 Meta-analysis Electrophysiology n = 1,298,291 impact 3/5

Physical activity, atrial fibrillation and the dose-response relationship: a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Davies CL et al. · PubMed

In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 37 studies (n = 1,298,291), physical activity was associated with 22% lower odds of atrial fibrillation in the general population (OR 0.78; 95% CI 0.68, 0.89) while athlete status was associated with substantially higher odds (OR 2.74; 95% CI 1.72, 4.39); spline modelling placed the greatest benefit at 26 MET-hr/wk and a reversal to increased odds beyond 78 MET-hr/wk, roughly 6-7 hours per week of endurance training.

Design
Systematic review and meta-analysis with weighted random-effects pooling and a weighted restricted cubic spline dose-response model of logarithmic odds; four databases searched from January 1990 to June 2025.
Population
37 included studies comprising 1,298,291 individuals, from general populations through to athletes; the dose-response analysis used 26 studies (n = 1,022,790).
Intervention
Physical activity exposure, standardised across studies to metabolic equivalent of task hours per week (MET-hr/wk), including athlete status as a separate exposure category.
Comparison
Sedentary control groups within each included study.
Outcomes
Atrial fibrillation incidence or prevalence, expressed as pooled odds ratios. In the general population, physical activity was associated with a 22% reduction in odds of AF (22 studies; OR 0.78; 95% CI 0.68, 0.89). Athlete status was associated with increased odds of AF (15 studies; OR 2.74; 95% CI 1.72, 4.39). P values were not reported.

Other results

  • General population: physical activity associated with 22% lower odds of atrial fibrillation (22 studies; OR 0.78; 95% CI 0.68, 0.89).
  • Athlete status: higher odds of atrial fibrillation (15 studies; OR 2.74; 95% CI 1.72, 4.39).
  • Dose-response (26 studies; n = 1,022,790): greatest reduction in odds of AF at 26 MET-hr/wk of physical activity.
  • Doses beyond 78 MET-hr/wk were associated with increased odds of AF, described by the authors as the equivalent of 6-7 hours per week of endurance training.
  • The overall relationship was U-shaped across the full continuum of exposure.

Strengths

  • Very large pooled sample (1,298,291 individuals across 37 studies), giving precision at both ends of the exposure range.
  • Exposures standardised to MET-hr/wk, allowing heterogeneous activity measures to be placed on one quantitative scale and producing an actionable numeric threshold.
  • Spline modelling of the full continuum rather than category comparisons, which is the only way to locate the point at which the curve reverses.
  • Broad search across four databases over a 35-year window, with athletes analysed separately from general populations.
  • Addresses a question patients ask directly - how much endurance exercise is too much - and translates the threshold into hours of training per week.

Limitations

  • Pooling of observational studies only: the associations cannot establish that high exercise volume causes atrial fibrillation, and residual confounding (height, body size, alcohol, sleep apnoea, hypertension) is likely.
  • Both incidence and prevalence studies were combined and results are reported as odds ratios, mixing study designs and inviting bias in the pooled estimate.
  • Detection bias is a major concern at the extremes: athletes are more monitored and more symptom-aware, and AF ascertainment methods are likely to differ between athlete cohorts and general populations.
  • Physical activity is overwhelmingly self-reported in epidemiological cohorts, so the MET-hr/wk conversion carries substantial measurement error, and the 26 and 78 MET-hr/wk thresholds are modelled point estimates presented without confidence intervals.
  • Heterogeneity statistics (I2), P values, publication-bias assessment, risk-of-bias ratings and whether the review was prospectively registered are not reported in the abstract.
  • The athlete estimate is imprecise (95% CI 1.72, 4.39) and 'athlete' is a heterogeneous category spanning very different sports, sexes, ages and training histories.
  • Funding is not reported.

Practice implications

This supports the standard advice that regular moderate activity protects against atrial fibrillation, and provides a number to use in conversation: benefit appears maximal around 26 MET-hr/wk, with a signal of increased AF odds beyond roughly 78 MET-hr/wk or 6-7 hours per week of endurance training. Because the evidence is observational and vulnerable to detection bias, it should temper rather than forbid high-volume training: a reason to discuss AF risk, symptoms and modifiable co-factors such as alcohol with endurance athletes, and reassurance that nothing here argues against recommending ordinary exercise volumes to patients at risk of AF.

Eur J Heart Fail 2026-10-17 Meta-analysis Heart failure n = 26,948 impact 3/5

Diagnostic Accuracy of NT-proBNP thresholds in Dyspneic Patients in the Emergency Department according to patients' characteristics: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.

Baudry G et al. · PubMed

In a meta-analysis of 31 studies (26,948 dyspneic emergency department patients), NT-proBNP 300 ng/L or below ruled out acute heart failure with 97.9% (96.9-98.5) sensitivity and a 97.0% (96.5-97.4) negative predictive value, while age-dependent rule-in thresholds (450, 900 and 1,800 ng/L for ages <50, 50-75 and 75+ years) outperformed fixed thresholds (AUC 0.84 [0.83-0.85] vs 0.73 [0.69-0.77]); performance fell in chronic kidney disease (AUC 0.77 [0.75-0.78]).

Design
Systematic review and meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy studies published up to 28 August 2025.
Population
31 studies comprising 26,948 patients presenting to the emergency department with dyspnoea. Subgroup analyses included age-dependent thresholds in 14,410 patients, chronic kidney disease in 2,271 patients and obesity (body mass index 30 or above) in 3,562 patients.
Intervention
NT-proBNP measurement interpreted against fixed thresholds (including 300 ng/L or below for rule-out) and against age-dependent thresholds of 450 ng/L or above for patients aged under 50 years, 900 ng/L or above for ages 50-75 years, and 1,800 ng/L or above for ages 75 years and over.
Comparison
Reference standard diagnosis of acute heart failure as adjudicated in each included study; fixed versus age-dependent threshold strategies were compared against one another.
Outcomes
Diagnostic accuracy (AUC, sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value). Rule-out threshold 300 ng/L or below: AUC 0.73 (0.69-0.77), sensitivity 97.9% (96.9-98.5), specificity 46.2% (36.4-56.2), negative predictive value 97.0% (96.5-97.4). Age-dependent thresholds: AUC 0.84 (0.83-0.85), sensitivity 85.9% (85.0-86.7), specificity 82.0% (81.2-82.9). P values were not reported.

Other results

  • Rule-out with NT-proBNP 300 ng/L or below: AUC 0.73 (0.69-0.77), sensitivity 97.9% (96.9-98.5), specificity 46.2% (36.4-56.2), negative predictive value 97.0% (96.5-97.4).
  • Age-dependent thresholds (450 ng/L if aged <50 years, 900 ng/L if 50-75 years, 1,800 ng/L if 75 years or over), assessed in 14,410 patients: AUC 0.84 (0.83-0.85), sensitivity 85.9% (85.0-86.7), specificity 82.0% (81.2-82.9).
  • Chronic kidney disease (n = 2,271): all fixed thresholds gave AUC below 0.80; age-dependent thresholds gave AUC 0.77 (0.75-0.78) with specificity only 62.6% (59.4-65.7).
  • Obesity with body mass index 30 or above (n = 3,562, age-dependent thresholds only): AUC 0.82 (0.81-0.84), sensitivity 81.2% (79.2-83.1), broadly comparable to the overall population.
  • The authors conclude that 300 ng/L or below is an excellent rule-out threshold while age-dependent thresholds are superior for ruling in acute heart failure.

Strengths

  • Large pooled diagnostic dataset (31 studies, 26,948 patients) addressing the exact decision made at the front door in dyspnoea.
  • Evaluates both rule-out and rule-in strategies rather than a single cut-point, and reports sensitivity, specificity, AUC and negative predictive value with confidence intervals.
  • Prespecified clinically important subgroups (chronic kidney disease, obesity) in which natriuretic peptide interpretation is known to be problematic.
  • Directly validates the age-stratified thresholds already embedded in guidelines, giving quantitative support for a strategy many clinicians already use.
  • Confirms the high negative predictive value (97.0%) that underpins safe discharge decisions in low-probability patients.
  • Recent search date (August 2025), so contemporary assays and practice are represented.

Limitations

  • Meta-analysis of diagnostic accuracy studies with heterogeneous reference standards for acute heart failure adjudication, which is itself imperfect and often informed by the biomarker under test (incorporation bias).
  • Pooled sensitivity and specificity depend heavily on disease prevalence and spectrum in the contributing emergency departments, so the reported negative predictive value will not transfer to settings with very different pretest probability.
  • Heterogeneity statistics (I2), risk-of-bias assessment (for example QUADAS-2), publication-bias testing and whether the review was prospectively registered are not reported in the abstract.
  • Subgroup evidence is thin: only 2,271 patients with chronic kidney disease and 3,562 with obesity, with obesity assessed only for age-dependent thresholds and no renal-function-specific thresholds tested.
  • Specificity of the 300 ng/L rule-out threshold is poor (46.2%), so this cut-point alone leaves a large indeterminate group, and the intermediate 'grey zone' is not characterised.
  • Confidence intervals around some pooled estimates are implausibly narrow for aggregated diagnostic data, suggesting fixed-effect-like pooling or within-study correlation that may understate uncertainty.
  • Assay platforms, timing of sampling relative to presentation, and treatment before sampling are not described; P values and funding are not reported.

Practice implications

This supports current practice: use NT-proBNP 300 ng/L or below to rule out acute heart failure in the dyspneic emergency department patient, where the 97.0% negative predictive value makes an alternative diagnosis the safer working assumption, and use the age-stratified cut-points (450, 900 and 1,800 ng/L) rather than a single fixed rule-in value. The caution is renal dysfunction, where accuracy drops and specificity falls to about 63% - here a raised NT-proBNP should prompt imaging and clinical correlation rather than a diagnosis. Obesity, reassuringly, did not materially degrade performance of the age-dependent thresholds.

Eur J Heart Fail 2026-10-15 Meta-analysis Heart failure impact 3/5

Sex-Based Differences in Chronic Heart Failure: a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.

Fazzini L et al. · PubMed

Abstract

BACKGROUND: Sex-based disparities in patient enrollment and treatment effects in heart failure (HF) randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been reported. We aim to systematically analyze sex-based enrollment and treatment effect differences in HF RCTs over time. METHODS: RCTs enrolling patients with chronic HF were systematically searched on PubMed-MEDLINE. Eligible trials included pharmacological or device-based interventions with a hard primary endpoint (mortality and hospitalization) and reporting enrollment of women. Sex-specific treatment effects (HR or RR) were extracted. Meta-analyses were performed using random-effects models, and pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed. RESULTS: A total of 56 RCTs (39 drug, 17 device) were included. Development and Validation of an Adult Medical Urgency Score for US Donor Heart Allocation.

Lyden GR et al. · PubMed

Using US transplant registry data (training n=21,859; validation n=7,897; test n=8,256), a continuous medical urgency score (US-CRS) built from 5 laboratory values and 4 support devices discriminated 6-week mortality without transplant far better than the current 6-status system (mean time-dependent AUC 0.807 vs 0.693) and, in simulation, would place sicker candidates at the top of the match run (observed 6-week transplant-censored mortality for the first candidate 15.7% [95% CI, 13.9%-17.6%] vs 11.2% [95% CI, 10.1%-12.5%]).

Design
Registry-based observational development and validation study with chronologically split training (2019-2023), validation (2023-2024) and test (2024-2025) datasets, a marginal structural model of 6-week mortality without transplant, comparison of 8 candidate models in held-out data, and a match-run simulation of waitlist reordering.
Population
All adult (aged 18 years or older) US heart transplant candidates from January 2019 to August 2025: training n=21,859, validation n=7,897, test n=8,256 (mean [SD] age, 53 [13] years; 74% male). Match-run analysis covered 45,530 donor hearts.
Intervention
Ranking of candidates by the US Candidate Risk Score (US-CRS), a continuous medical urgency score comprising 5 laboratory values and 4 therapeutic devices (durable left ventricular assist device, intra-aortic balloon pump, microaxial flow pump, and advanced mechanical circulatory support).
Comparison
The current 6-status, therapy-based US donor heart allocation system.
Outcomes
Six-week mortality without transplant, assessed by weighted time-dependent AUC and calibration by status. In test-set cross-sections of candidates waiting on the same calendar date, US-CRS had similar or significantly higher time-dependent AUC than the 6-status system (mean AUC, 0.807 vs 0.693). In the match-run simulation, observed 6-week transplant-censored mortality for the first candidate on the match run rose from 11.2% (95% CI, 10.1%-12.5%) under the current system to 15.7% (95% CI, 13.9%-17.6%) under US-CRS.

Other results

  • Mean time-dependent AUC for 6-week mortality without transplant: 0.807 with US-CRS vs 0.693 with the current 6-status system in test-set cross-sections of candidates actively waiting on the same calendar date.
  • US-CRS showed acceptable calibration by status, especially for candidates who received their status by exception - the group the current system handles worst.
  • The selected model used 5 laboratory values and 4 therapeutic devices (durable LVAD, intra-aortic balloon pump, microaxial flow pump, advanced mechanical circulatory support).
  • Match-run simulation across 45,530 donor hearts: 67% of transplant recipients moved down in rank order under a US-CRS-based policy.
  • Observed 6-week transplant-censored mortality of the first candidate on the match run: 15.7% (95% CI, 13.9%-17.6%) under US-CRS vs 11.2% (95% CI, 10.1%-12.5%) under the current system, indicating the score puts higher-risk candidates first.
  • Eight candidate models were compared in held-out data before the final score was chosen.

Strengths

  • National registry coverage of all adult US candidates over 2019-2025, so the development population is the actual target population for the policy.
  • Chronological rather than random data splitting, with a test set (2024-2025) later than the development data, which tests robustness to practice drift and to the 2018 allocation-policy era.
  • A marginal structural model appropriately handles the time-varying nature of therapy and the competing event of transplant, rather than modelling mortality at listing only.
  • Evaluation goes beyond discrimination to calibration by status and to a concrete match-run simulation of 45,530 donor hearts, translating a statistical model into policy consequences.
  • Built from objective laboratory values and device support rather than therapy-based tiers, directly targeting the gaming and exception-request problems of the current 6-status system.
  • Large absolute improvement in discrimination (AUC 0.807 vs 0.693) that is unlikely to be explained by overfitting given held-out testing.

Limitations

  • Observational registry study: the benefit is demonstrated as better risk ranking, not as improved waitlist or post-transplant survival, which no simulation can establish.
  • Six-week mortality without transplant is a surrogate for the real objective of maximizing survival benefit; prioritizing the sickest candidates may not maximize post-transplant survival or total life-years, and post-transplant outcomes were not modelled.
  • Registry data are subject to missing and misreported laboratory values and to centre-level variation in practice; the score's inputs (e.g. device use) remain partly under centre control and could themselves be gamed.
  • The match-run simulation assumes unchanged candidate listing and acceptance behaviour, which would almost certainly change under a new policy.
  • Score performance in important subgroups (women, congenital heart disease, amyloidosis, retransplant, candidates on durable LVAD support) and the identity of the 5 laboratory values are not reported in the abstract.
  • Calibration was assessed by current status categories, and the trade-off of 67% of recipients moving down in rank order - including which groups lose priority - is not quantified in the abstract.
  • Funding and sponsor role are not reported.

Practice implications

For clinicians who list patients for heart transplantation, this is the evidence base behind the expected move from the 6-status tiers to a continuous US-CRS-based urgency score. Practically, allocation priority would shift toward objective physiology (laboratory values) interacting with the type of mechanical support, reducing the value of status-by-exception requests and of therapy choices made to secure a tier. Listing practice, documentation of laboratory data and the timing of device escalation would all become more consequential, while candidates currently advantaged by therapy-based tiering - 67% of recipients moved down in rank in simulation - may wait longer.

Abstract
