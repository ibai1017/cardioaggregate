N Engl J Med 2026-11-07 RCT Hypertension n = 4,368 impact 5/5

Intensive Blood-Pressure Control in Patients with Previous Ischemic Stroke.

Sampaio Silva G et al. · PubMed

In 4368 Brazilian patients with elevated blood pressure and a previous ischemic stroke or TIA, targeting systolic blood pressure below 120 mm Hg rather than below 140 mm Hg reduced major cardiovascular events by about a fifth over a median 3.9 years (HR 0.79; 95% CI 0.67 to 0.94; P = 0.007), at the cost of more renal events.

Design
Multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial at 30 sites in Brazil (OPTIMAL-STROKE, NCT04036409).
Population
4368 participants with elevated blood pressure and previous ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack; mean age 63.9 +/- 11.2 years, 2093 (47.9%) women.
Intervention
Intensive antihypertensive treatment targeting systolic blood pressure less than 120 mm Hg (n = 2172).
Comparison
Standard antihypertensive treatment targeting systolic blood pressure less than 140 mm Hg (n = 2196).
Outcomes
Primary outcome was a composite of death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure: 236 participants (10.9%; 3.25 events per 100 person-years) with intensive treatment versus 297 (13.5%; 4.11 events per 100 person-years) with standard treatment; hazard ratio 0.79 (95% CI, 0.67 to 0.94; P = 0.007) over a median follow-up of 3.9 years.

Other results

  • Primary composite outcome: 236/2172 (10.9%; 3.25 per 100 person-years) intensive versus 297/2196 (13.5%; 4.11 per 100 person-years) standard; HR 0.79 (95% CI, 0.67 to 0.94; P = 0.007).
  • Achieved mean systolic blood pressure after the first 3 months was 122.0 +/- 11.5 mm Hg (median 119.6) with intensive treatment versus 135.2 +/- 9.7 mm Hg (median 135.0) with standard treatment, a between-group difference of 13.1 mm Hg (95% CI, 12.5 to 13.8).
  • The composite renal outcome (sustained reduction of at least 50% in eGFR or progression to end-stage renal disease) was more common with intensive treatment; event numbers and effect estimates not reported in the abstract.
  • Overall incidence of serious adverse events was similar in the two groups; numbers not reported.
  • Median follow-up 3.9 years; 4368 participants randomized (2172 intensive, 2196 standard).

Strengths

  • Randomized design with a large sample (4368) and an event-driven hard clinical composite including cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction and stroke.
  • A large and sustained separation in achieved systolic blood pressure (13.1 mm Hg; 95% CI, 12.5 to 13.8) confirms the trial actually tested the intended contrast.
  • Median follow-up of 3.9 years is long enough for the composite endpoint to accrue (533 events in total).
  • Addresses a population specifically excluded or under-represented in SPRINT, where post-stroke blood-pressure targets have remained uncertain.
  • Publicly funded (Hospital Israelita Albert Einstein and the Brazilian Ministry of Health) rather than industry sponsored.

Limitations

  • Open-label treatment assignment; although the primary endpoint components are relatively objective, ascertainment and management bias cannot be excluded and blinding of outcome adjudication is not described in the abstract.
  • Conducted entirely at 30 sites in a single country (Brazil), which limits generalizability to other health systems and populations.
  • The renal signal is clinically important but unquantified here: the abstract reports only that the composite renal outcome was more common with intensive treatment, with no event numbers, effect estimate or confidence interval.
  • No breakdown of the individual components of the primary composite, and no data on hypotension, syncope, falls or electrolyte abnormalities, which are the usual trade-offs of a target below 120 mm Hg.
  • Baseline blood pressure, time from the index stroke or TIA, stroke subtypes and the drug regimens used are not reported, so it is unclear which post-stroke patients and which treatment approach the result applies to.

Practice implications

Supports pursuing a systolic target below 120 mm Hg in patients with prior ischemic stroke or TIA who tolerate it, with a number needed to treat of roughly 38 over 3.9 years for the composite endpoint. Because the renal composite was more frequent with intensive treatment, monitor eGFR during titration and weigh the strategy against baseline kidney function; the unreported magnitude of that renal harm and the single-country, open-label design argue for individualized targets rather than blanket adoption.

N Engl J Med 2026-11-07 RCT Prevention and lipids n = 8,322 impact 5/5

Pelacarsen and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients with High Lipoprotein(a).

Cho L et al. · PubMed

In 8322 patients with established cardiovascular disease and lipoprotein(a) of at least 70 mg/dL, monthly pelacarsen lowered Lp(a) by roughly 72% yet produced no reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events over a median 4.7 years (12.3% versus 12.1%; HR 1.02; 95% CI, 0.90 to 1.15; P = 0.76), a decisively null result for the first large Lp(a)-lowering outcome trial.

Design
International, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled cardiovascular outcome trial (Lp[a]HORIZON, NCT04023552), funded by Novartis.
Population
8350 randomized patients (8322 in the efficacy analysis) with preexisting cardiovascular disease and a lipoprotein(a) level of at least 70 mg per deciliter; mean age 59.7 years, 27.0% women, median baseline Lp(a) 236 nmol per liter.
Intervention
Subcutaneous pelacarsen 80 mg monthly (n = 4157).
Comparison
Matching placebo monthly (n = 4165).
Outcomes
Primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (composite of death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or urgent coronary revascularization): 511 patients (12.3%) with pelacarsen versus 503 (12.1%) with placebo; hazard ratio 1.02 (95% CI, 0.90 to 1.15; P = 0.76) over a median follow-up of 4.7 years.

Other results

  • Primary MACE composite: 511/4157 (12.3%) pelacarsen versus 503/4165 (12.1%) placebo; HR 1.02 (95% CI, 0.90 to 1.15; P = 0.76).
  • Among patients with baseline Lp(a) of at least 90 mg per deciliter: 402 of 3266 (12.3%) pelacarsen versus 403 of 3274 (12.3%) placebo; HR 1.00 (95% CI, 0.87 to 1.15; P = 0.99).
  • Target engagement was substantial: at 12 months Lp(a) was 64.4 nmol per liter with pelacarsen versus 233.4 nmol per liter with placebo (median baseline 236 nmol per liter).
  • Serious adverse events occurred in 1553 of 4166 (37.3%) pelacarsen recipients versus 1709 of 4154 (41.1%) placebo recipients.
  • Median follow-up 4.7 years; 1014 primary endpoint events in total.

Strengths

  • Large (8350 randomized), international, double-blind, placebo-controlled design with hard clinical endpoints and 1014 accrued events.
  • Unambiguous target engagement: Lp(a) fell to 64.4 from a median baseline of 236 nmol per liter, so the null result reflects the biology rather than inadequate drug effect.
  • Confidence interval is narrow and excludes any clinically meaningful benefit (upper bound of risk reduction only 10%), making this a genuinely informative negative trial rather than an underpowered one.
  • Enriched population (Lp(a) at least 70 mg/dL, established cardiovascular disease) with a prespecified very-high-Lp(a) stratum that was also flatly null (HR 1.00; 95% CI, 0.87 to 1.15).
  • Long median follow-up of 4.7 years, adequate for an atherosclerosis-modifying mechanism to declare itself.

Limitations

  • Background therapy, LDL cholesterol levels and statin or PCSK9 inhibitor use are not reported in the abstract, so it is unclear whether intensive background lipid lowering left little room for incremental benefit.
  • Industry funded (Novartis); the abstract gives no information on trial governance or independent endpoint adjudication.
  • Only the composite and one Lp(a) subgroup are reported here, with no data on individual endpoint components, so a differential effect on, for example, revascularization versus death cannot be assessed.
  • Patients were relatively young (mean 59.7 years) and only 27.0% were women; event rates were modest (about 12% over 4.7 years), which may limit extrapolation to older or higher-risk patients.
  • A null result in secondary prevention over 4.7 years does not exclude benefit from lowering Lp(a) much earlier in life or over much longer exposure, and injection-site or other adverse events are not detailed.

Practice implications

There is now no outcome evidence to support Lp(a) lowering with an antisense oligonucleotide for secondary prevention, and pelacarsen should not be anticipated as a therapy. Measuring Lp(a) remains useful for risk stratification and family screening, but an elevated level should prompt intensification of proven therapies (LDL cholesterol lowering, blood pressure control, antithrombotic therapy) rather than referral for Lp(a)-directed treatment. Await the ongoing trials of other agents in this class before concluding the Lp(a) hypothesis is dead, but set patient expectations accordingly.

N Engl J Med 2026-11-08 RCT Electrophysiology n = 685 impact 5/5

Left Bundle-Branch Pacing versus Right Ventricular Pacing for Atrioventricular Block.

Qian Z et al. · PubMed

In 685 patients paced for atrioventricular block, left bundle-branch pacing more than halved the composite of death, heart-failure hospitalization or pacing-induced cardiomyopathy compared with right ventricular pacing (4-year cumulative incidence 8.5% versus 19.2%; HR 0.41; 95% CI, 0.23 to 0.75; P = 0.003), and the trial was stopped early for benefit.

Design
Investigator-initiated, international, open-label, randomized controlled trial, terminated early at a prespecified interim analysis on the recommendation of the data monitoring committee (OptimPacing, NCT04624763).
Population
702 patients with atrioventricular block randomized; 685 analysed in the modified intention-to-treat population (342 left bundle-branch pacing, 343 right ventricular pacing). Mean age 68.4 years, 57.5% men.
Intervention
Left bundle-branch pacing (348 assigned; 342 analysed).
Comparison
Conventional right ventricular pacing (354 assigned; 343 analysed).
Outcomes
Primary outcome was a composite of death from any cause, hospitalization for heart failure, or pacing-induced cardiomyopathy: 16 events with left bundle-branch pacing versus 35 with right ventricular pacing (Kaplan-Meier 4-year cumulative incidence 8.5% versus 19.2%); hazard ratio 0.41 (95% CI, 0.23 to 0.75; P = 0.003 by stratified log-rank test) at a median follow-up of 35.1 months (IQR, 23.6 to 40.7).

Other results

  • Primary composite: 16 events in the left bundle-branch pacing group versus 35 in the right ventricular pacing group; 4-year Kaplan-Meier cumulative incidence 8.5% versus 19.2%; HR 0.41 (95% CI, 0.23 to 0.75; P = 0.003).
  • The data monitoring committee recommended early termination at the prespecified interim analysis because of benefit.
  • Serious adverse events unrelated to the implantation procedure: 39 patients (11.8%) with left bundle-branch pacing versus 33 (9.3%) with right ventricular pacing (P = 0.32).
  • 17 patients (6 left bundle-branch, 11 right ventricular) were excluded before device implantation and were not analysed.
  • Median follow-up 35.1 months (IQR, 23.6 to 40.7); contributions of the individual components (death, heart-failure hospitalization, pacing-induced cardiomyopathy) to the composite are not reported.

Strengths

  • Randomized comparison with clinically meaningful endpoints rather than the electrical or echocardiographic surrogates on which conduction-system pacing has largely been justified until now.
  • Investigator-initiated and multinational, reducing the risk of device-industry influence on design and reporting.
  • Median follow-up of 35.1 months is long enough for pacing-induced cardiomyopathy to develop, which typically takes years.
  • Effect size is large and consistent in direction with the mechanistic rationale of preserving ventricular synchrony, and the confidence interval excludes equivalence.
  • Procedural and non-procedural serious adverse events were reported separately, showing no significant excess with the newer technique (11.8% versus 9.3%; P = 0.32).

Limitations

  • Stopped early for benefit at an interim analysis, which systematically overestimates treatment effect; the true hazard ratio is likely closer to 1 than 0.41, and the 95% CI (0.23 to 0.75) is wide.
  • Only 51 primary events in total (16 versus 35), so the estimate rests on small absolute numbers and the component breakdown cannot be assessed.
  • Open-label design: pacing-induced cardiomyopathy is a partly adjudicated, echocardiographically defined endpoint, and heart-failure hospitalization is clinician-driven, so unblinded ascertainment could inflate the difference.
  • Baseline left ventricular ejection fraction, expected ventricular pacing burden and the definition and adjudication process for pacing-induced cardiomyopathy are not reported, so the patients most likely to benefit cannot be identified.
  • Procedural complications specific to left bundle-branch lead placement (septal perforation, lead dislodgement, long-term lead performance and extractability) are not reported here, and results come from experienced implanters, which may not generalize to lower-volume centres.

Practice implications

This is the first randomized clinical-outcome evidence favouring left bundle-branch area pacing over right ventricular pacing in atrioventricular block, and it supports making conduction-system pacing the default for patients expected to need a high ventricular pacing burden, where operator expertise exists. Temper expectations about the magnitude of benefit because the trial stopped early on 51 events, and counsel patients that long-term lead performance and extractability data are still lacking.

N Engl J Med 2026-11-12 RCT Thrombosis and vascular n = 15,317 impact 4/5

Prevention of Venous Thromboembolism after Total Hip or Knee Replacement.

Pellegrini VD et al. · PubMed

In 15,317 patients undergoing elective hip or knee arthroplasty, aspirin 81 mg twice daily failed to establish noninferiority to rivaroxaban or warfarin for death or symptomatic VTE, and neither anticoagulant was noninferior to aspirin for safety; absolute differences were small (all well under 1 percentage point) and every confidence interval crossed zero, so no agent emerged as clearly superior.

Design
Randomized, three-arm, comparative-effectiveness noninferiority trial with blinded 6-month outcome evaluation; noninferiority margin for the risk difference 0.69 percentage points (PEPPER, NCT02810704), funded by the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute.
Population
18,883 participants randomized after elective hip or knee arthroplasty; 15,317 in the primary per-protocol analysis (7214 hip, 8103 knee).
Intervention
Aspirin 81 mg twice daily for 30 days (6090 in the per-protocol analysis).
Comparison
Warfarin to a target INR of 2.0 (4231) or rivaroxaban 10 mg daily (4996), each for 30 days.
Outcomes
Primary effectiveness outcome (death from any cause or symptomatic VTE) through 6 months: 77 of 6090 (1.26%) with aspirin, 52 of 4231 (1.23%) with warfarin, 47 of 4996 (0.94%) with rivaroxaban. Primary safety outcome (bleeding, reoperation for wound problems, or myocardial infarction): 177 (2.91%), 142 (3.36%) and 174 (3.48%) respectively. Pairwise effectiveness: aspirin versus rivaroxaban 1.24% versus 0.94% (difference 0.30 percentage points; 95% CI, -0.20 to 0.81); aspirin versus warfarin 1.24% versus 1.01% (difference 0.23 percentage points; 95% CI, -0.34 to 0.81); warfarin versus rivaroxaban 1.13% versus 1.10% (difference 0.03 percentage points; 95% CI, -0.57 to 0.63).

Other results

  • Effectiveness event rates: aspirin 77/6090 (1.26%), warfarin 52/4231 (1.23%), rivaroxaban 47/4996 (0.94%).
  • Aspirin was not noninferior to rivaroxaban (difference 0.30 percentage points; 95% CI, -0.20 to 0.81) or to warfarin (difference 0.23 percentage points; 95% CI, -0.34 to 0.81) against a 0.69 percentage point margin, because the upper confidence bounds exceeded the margin.
  • Warfarin versus rivaroxaban for effectiveness: 1.13% versus 1.10% (difference 0.03 percentage points; 95% CI, -0.57 to 0.63).
  • Safety event rates: aspirin 177 (2.91%), warfarin 142 (3.36%), rivaroxaban 174 (3.48%). Rivaroxaban versus aspirin difference 0.68 percentage points (95% CI, -0.04 to 1.41); warfarin versus aspirin difference 0.77 percentage points (95% CI, -0.04 to 1.58); rivaroxaban versus warfarin difference -0.02 percentage points (95% CI, -0.89 to 0.85).
  • Neither warfarin nor rivaroxaban was noninferior to aspirin for safety; intention-to-treat analyses yielded similar results.

Strengths

  • Very large pragmatic randomized trial (18,883 randomized) directly comparing the three strategies actually used in practice, with blinded outcome evaluation through 6 months.
  • Publicly funded by PCORI rather than by a drug manufacturer, and designed as a comparative-effectiveness study of real clinical choices.
  • Both effectiveness and safety were prespecified as primary outcomes and tested in both directions, which is the honest way to frame a trade-off question.
  • Intention-to-treat analyses were consistent with the per-protocol results, which matters because per-protocol analysis is the more fragile choice in a noninferiority design.
  • Event rates are reported with absolute risks and risk differences, letting clinicians see that all strategies produce low absolute event rates (effectiveness about 1%, safety about 3%).

Limitations

  • The headline conclusions are failures to demonstrate noninferiority, not demonstrations of difference: every pairwise confidence interval includes zero, so the trial is formally inconclusive rather than showing that any agent is worse.
  • The 0.69 percentage point noninferiority margin is narrow relative to the observed event rates, so the trial was effectively asking whether the agents are nearly identical; with 15,317 patients it still could not resolve differences of that size.
  • Primary analysis was per-protocol; adherence rates, crossover and the amount of exclusion between the 18,883 randomized and 15,317 analysed (about 19%) are not reported in the abstract and could bias a noninferiority comparison.
  • The composite safety outcome bundles bleeding, reoperation for wound problems and myocardial infarction, which have very different clinical weights, and no component-level data are given.
  • Open-label treatment is implied (warfarin requires INR titration), aspirin was given at 81 mg twice daily, which is not the regimen used everywhere, and prophylaxis lasted only 30 days, so the findings may not transfer to other doses or durations.

Practice implications

For cardiologists asked to advise on thromboprophylaxis after hip or knee arthroplasty, this trial does not dethrone aspirin but it does remove the comfortable assumption that aspirin is as effective as anticoagulation: symptomatic VTE or death was numerically about 0.3 percentage points more common with aspirin than rivaroxaban, while composite safety events were about 0.7 percentage points more common with each anticoagulant. The trade-off is small in absolute terms and runs in opposite directions, so the choice can reasonably be individualized by VTE risk, bleeding and wound risk, and the practicalities of monitoring, rather than driven by a presumption that the agents are interchangeable.

N Engl J Med 2026-11-08 RCT Acute and critical care n = 22,459 impact 4/5

Community and EMS Interventions to Improve Survival in Cardiac Arrest.

Malta Hansen C et al. · PubMed

In a cluster-randomized trial across North Carolina counties involving 22,447 analysed out-of-hospital cardiac arrests, a four-component community and EMS intervention did not improve survival to discharge with good neurologic outcome (9.0% versus 9.4%; adjusted OR 0.97; 95% CI, 0.75 to 1.26; P = 0.80).

Design
Registry-based, cluster-randomized trial; North Carolina counties grouped into clusters and randomized 1:1 to the intervention or usual care, with outcomes captured through the Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival (RACE-CARS, NCT04660526), funded by the NHLBI.
Population
Adults with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest treated by EMS between 1 July 2022 and 30 June 2026: 28 clusters comprising 11,615 patients (intervention) and 27 clusters comprising 10,844 patients (usual care). Median age 64.0 years, 36.7% women.
Intervention
A multifaceted strategy with four components: training 911 telecommunicators to recognize cardiac arrest rapidly, training them to deliver bystander CPR instructions, community CPR training, and enhanced use of automated external defibrillators by first responders or bystanders before EMS arrival.
Comparison
Usual care in the control clusters.
Outcomes
Primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge with a good neurologic outcome (Cerebral Performance Category 1 or 2): 1048 of 11,608 patients (9.0%) in the intervention group versus 1017 of 10,839 (9.4%) in the usual-care group; adjusted odds ratio 0.97 (95% CI, 0.75 to 1.26; P = 0.80).

Other results

  • Primary outcome: 1048/11,608 (9.0%) intervention versus 1017/10,839 (9.4%) usual care; adjusted OR 0.97 (95% CI, 0.75 to 1.26; P = 0.80).
  • 55 clusters in total (28 intervention, 27 usual care) covering 22,459 assigned patients over a four-year enrolment period.
  • Median patient age 64.0 years; 36.7% women.
  • No improvement was seen despite a bundle that targeted every link in the early chain of survival (recognition, dispatcher-assisted CPR, community CPR training, early AED use).
  • Process measures (rates of bystander CPR, telecommunicator recognition, AED application) and secondary outcomes such as overall survival or return of spontaneous circulation are not reported in the abstract.

Strengths

  • Randomized evidence for a question almost always addressed with before-and-after or observational designs, which are badly confounded by secular trends in cardiac arrest care.
  • Very large (22,447 analysed patients across 55 clusters) with near-complete outcome capture through an established registry, and an endpoint that matters to patients (survival with good neurologic function) rather than return of spontaneous circulation.
  • Analysis appropriately accounts for clustering, and the four-year enrolment period spans enough time for the implementation to take hold.
  • Publicly funded by the NHLBI with no commercial interest in the result.
  • The intervention bundled the components that observational data and guidelines most strongly endorse, so this is a fair test of the prevailing community strategy.

Limitations

  • Implementation fidelity is not reported here: without data on whether bystander CPR rates, telecommunicator recognition or AED use actually improved, a null result cannot be separated from a failure to deliver the intervention.
  • A bundle of four co-administered components cannot tell us which element, if any, works; a component that helps could be masked by others that do not.
  • Cluster-randomized designs with 55 units have limited power to detect modest effects, and the confidence interval (0.75 to 1.26) does not exclude a clinically relevant benefit or harm.
  • Conducted in a single US state with an unusually mature cardiac arrest system (the RACE network), where usual care may already be close to best practice, limiting both the room to improve and generalizability to lower-performing systems.
  • Contamination between neighbouring counties, and secular improvements affecting both arms, would both bias toward a null result; the abstract reports no sensitivity analyses for either.

Practice implications

This well-powered randomized test undercuts the assumption that layering telecommunicator training, community CPR training and early AED promotion onto an already well-organized EMS system will improve neurologically intact survival. It is not a reason to abandon bystander CPR or public AEDs, whose individual benefit is well established, but it does argue against investing further in undifferentiated community bundles and in favour of measuring process fidelity, targeting the specific weak link in a given system, and looking to in-hospital and post-arrest care for additional gains.

BMJ 2026-11-11 RCT Hypertension n = 2,012 impact 4/5

Effect of discontinuing antihypertensives on perioperative hypotension in older patients before non-cardiac surgery: multicentre, randomised, double blind, controlled trial.

Yang Y et al. · PubMed

In 2012 patients aged 60-80 on chronic ACE inhibitor or ARB therapy undergoing non-cardiac surgery, withholding the drug on the morning of surgery cut perioperative hypotension from 45.5% to 31.2% (adjusted OR 0.52; 95% CI, 0.43 to 0.63; absolute risk difference -14.4%; P<0.001) and reduced vasopressor use, with no difference in 30-day complications or mortality.

Design
Multicentre, randomised, double blind, placebo controlled trial at five tertiary teaching hospitals in China, November 2020 to December 2023 (ChiCTR2000039376).
Population
2036 older patients (aged 60-80 years) taking an ACE inhibitor or ARB for at least two weeks and scheduled for non-cardiac surgery under general anaesthesia; 2012 completed the study (mean age 68.8 years, 54.4% male).
Intervention
Placebo in identical capsules on the morning of surgery, i.e. discontinuation of the ACE inhibitor or ARB (no antihypertensive group, n = 1008).
Comparison
The patient's usual ACE inhibitor or ARB on the morning of surgery (antihypertensive group, n = 1004).
Outcomes
Primary outcome was the incidence of perioperative hypotension (mean blood pressure <65 mm Hg or a decrease of at least 30% from baseline requiring treatment): 314 of 1008 (31.2%) with discontinuation versus 457 of 1004 (45.5%) with continuation; adjusted odds ratio 0.52 (95% CI, 0.43 to 0.63); absolute risk difference -14.4% (95% CI, -18.5% to -10.1%); P<0.001.

Other results

  • Perioperative hypotension: 314/1008 (31.2%) with discontinuation versus 457/1004 (45.5%) with continuation; adjusted OR 0.52 (95% CI, 0.43 to 0.63); absolute risk difference -14.4% (95% CI, -18.5% to -10.1%); P<0.001.
  • Vasopressor requirement: 28.5% versus 41.1%; adjusted OR 0.54 (95% CI, 0.44 to 0.66).
  • Duration of hypotension: adjusted mean difference -1.3 minutes (95% CI, -1.8 to -0.8 minutes) favouring discontinuation.
  • Intraoperative fluid volume, postoperative hypotension, 30 day complications (neurocognitive disorders, major cardiocerebral events) and mortality did not differ significantly between groups; event numbers and effect estimates not reported in the abstract.
  • 2036 randomised, 2012 (98.8%) completed the study, so attrition was minimal.

Strengths

  • Double blind and placebo controlled using identical capsules, which is unusual and difficult for a drug-withholding question and removes the ascertainment bias that plagues observational perioperative studies.
  • Large enough (2012 analysed) to produce a precise estimate for the primary outcome, with very low attrition (1.2%).
  • Addresses an extremely common and genuinely uncertain decision that cardiologists are asked about at nearly every preoperative consultation.
  • The primary outcome is a treatment-requiring haemodynamic event rather than a purely numerical blood pressure threshold, and the effect was corroborated by consistent reductions in vasopressor use and hypotension duration.
  • Prospectively registered with a prespecified primary outcome and a clinically coherent set of secondary outcomes including 30 day events.

Limitations

  • The primary outcome is an intermediate haemodynamic endpoint. Perioperative hypotension is associated with harm but the trial showed no difference in the outcomes that actually matter (30 day neurocognitive disorders, major cardiocerebral events, mortality), and it was almost certainly underpowered for them.
  • The absolute reduction in hypotension duration was only 1.3 minutes, which raises doubt about whether the hypotension prevented was clinically consequential.
  • Conducted at five Chinese tertiary teaching hospitals in patients aged 60-80, so it does not address younger or much older patients, non-tertiary settings, or populations with different anaesthetic practice and antihypertensive prescribing.
  • Only one preoperative dose was withheld; the trial cannot inform longer withholding strategies, nor patients on ACE inhibitors or ARBs for heart failure or proteinuric kidney disease, where continuation may matter more.
  • Baseline blood pressure, surgical types and risk, anaesthetic protocol and whether other antihypertensive classes were continued are not reported, and the absence of harm from withholding rests on unreported 30 day event counts.
  • The authors' claim of providing high certainty evidence for preoperative drug management overstates what a surrogate primary endpoint can support.

Practice implications

Reasonable support for routinely holding the morning dose of an ACE inhibitor or ARB in patients aged 60-80 having non-cardiac surgery under general anaesthesia, with roughly one episode of treatment-requiring hypotension avoided for every seven patients and no signal of harm at 30 days. Because the benefit is on a haemodynamic surrogate rather than on hard outcomes, the recommendation is conditional: continue to individualise for patients in whom these drugs are prescribed for heart failure or significant proteinuria, make sure the drug is restarted promptly postoperatively, and do not extrapolate to withholding for longer than the morning of surgery.

Lancet 2026-11-09 RCT Prevention and lipids n = 5,507 impact 4/5

Cardiovascular outcomes with survodutide in adults with obesity at increased cardiovascular risk (SYNCHRONIZE-CVOT): a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trial.

Bozkurt B et al. · PubMed

In 5507 adults with obesity at increased cardiovascular risk, the glucagon/GLP-1 dual agonist survodutide met its non-inferiority margin for cardiovascular safety over a median 22 months (3.7% versus 4.1%; HR 0.89; 95% CI, 0.67 to 1.18; p<0.0001 for non-inferiority), but gastrointestinal intolerance caused about one in five participants to stop treatment.

Design
Global, phase 3, randomised, double-blind, parallel-group, placebo-controlled, event-driven cardiovascular safety trial at 524 sites in 34 countries; non-inferiority margin 1.65 for the upper limit of the two-sided 95% CI of the hazard ratio, targeting 189 primary events (SYNCHRONIZE-CVOT, NCT06077864). Funded by Boehringer Ingelheim.
Population
5530 participants randomised (5507 treated) aged 18 years or older with BMI 27 kg/m2 or greater and established cardiovascular disease or at least two obesity complications or cardiovascular risk factors; those with chronic kidney disease required BMI 30 kg/m2 or greater. 2194 (39.8%) female, 3313 (60.2%) male, 4332 (78.7%) White.
Intervention
Once-weekly subcutaneous survodutide up-titrated to 3.6 mg (n = 1836) or 6.0 mg (n = 1835); the two doses were pooled (n = 3671) for the primary analysis.
Comparison
Matching placebo once weekly (n = 1836).
Outcomes
Primary endpoint was a composite of death from cardiovascular causes, non-fatal stroke, non-fatal myocardial infarction, ischaemia-related coronary revascularisation, or heart failure event: 136 of 3671 (3.7%) with pooled survodutide versus 76 of 1836 (4.1%) with placebo; HR 0.89 (95% CI, 0.67 to 1.18; p<0.0001 for non-inferiority) over a median time in study of 22.0 months (IQR 20.1-23.7).

Other results

  • Primary composite endpoint: 136/3671 (3.7%) pooled survodutide versus 76/1836 (4.1%) placebo; HR 0.89 (95% CI, 0.67 to 1.18; p<0.0001 for non-inferiority against a margin of 1.65).
  • Gastrointestinal adverse events were far more frequent with survodutide: nausea 882 (48.0%) with 3.6 mg and 900 (49.0%) with 6.0 mg versus 223 (12.1%) with placebo; vomiting 582 (31.7%) and 623 (34.0%) versus 89 (4.8%).
  • Gastrointestinal adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation: 354 (19.3%) with 3.6 mg, 381 (20.8%) with 6.0 mg, versus 34 (1.9%) with placebo.
  • Only 1029 (56.0%), 1039 (56.6%) and 1083 (59.0%) of the 3.6 mg, 6.0 mg and placebo groups respectively remained on treatment until study closeout.
  • Serious adverse events were similar across groups (413 [22.5%], 381 [20.8%], 439 [23.9%]), as were fatal adverse events (16 [0.9%], 16 [0.9%], 11 [0.6%]), mostly due to cardiac disorders (6 [0.3%], 7 [0.4%], 6 [0.3%]).

Strengths

  • Large, double-blind, placebo-controlled, event-driven design across 524 sites in 34 countries, which is the appropriate standard for a cardiovascular safety assessment of a new drug class.
  • Enrolled a genuinely at-risk population (established cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease or multiple risk factors), so events accrued and the safety question is answered in the patients who will receive the drug.
  • Primary analysis used a treatment-regimen estimand that counted events regardless of discontinuation or prohibited obesity medication use, which is the conservative choice for a safety question given the very high discontinuation rates.
  • Two doses were studied, with full adverse-event reporting by dose, letting clinicians see the dose-tolerability relationship.
  • Reported with complete safety accounting including serious and fatal adverse events and their causes.

Limitations

  • This is a safety non-inferiority trial, not an efficacy trial: it establishes the absence of excess cardiovascular risk but provides no evidence that survodutide reduces cardiovascular events, and the point estimate of 0.89 should not be read as benefit (the confidence interval extends to 1.18).
  • Median follow-up of only 22.0 months with 212 primary events is short for a chronic obesity therapy likely to be taken for years.
  • Striking tolerability problem: about 20% of survodutide recipients discontinued for gastrointestinal events and only around 56% remained on treatment at closeout, which both limits the exposure actually tested and raises doubt about real-world durability.
  • Industry funded (Boehringer Ingelheim), and no weight-loss, glycaemic or other efficacy outcomes are reported here, so the benefit side of the risk-benefit ledger cannot be judged from this paper.
  • Participants were predominantly White (78.7%) and the trial excluded BMI under 27 kg/m2, limiting generalisability; the heart failure component of the composite is not broken out, which matters for a glucagon receptor agonist.
  • Pooling two doses for the primary analysis can obscure a dose-dependent hazard.

Practice implications

Survodutide clears the regulatory cardiovascular safety bar, so cardiologists need not withhold it from patients with obesity and cardiovascular disease on cardiovascular-risk grounds. But this trial gives no cardiovascular benefit to offer patients, unlike the outcome data now available for semaglutide and tirzepatide, and the roughly 20% discontinuation rate for gastrointestinal events means dose escalation must be slow and expectations set accordingly. Until an efficacy outcome trial reports, prefer agents with proven cardiovascular benefit when the indication is cardiovascular risk reduction rather than weight loss alone.

Interleukin-6 Inhibition with Ziltivekimab for Atherosclerotic Disease.

Ridker PM et al. · PubMed

In 6376 patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease and hsCRP of at least 2 mg/L, monthly ziltivekimab reduced free interleukin-6 by 76% and hsCRP by 64% yet produced no reduction in 3-point MACE over a median 33 months (17.6% versus 17.7%; HR 0.99; 95% CI, 0.88 to 1.11; P = 0.84), with numerically more serious infections.

Design
Multinational, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled outcome trial (ZEUS, NCT05021835), funded by Novo Nordisk.
Population
6376 adults in the full analysis population with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular inflammation (high-sensitivity CRP at least 2 mg per liter); mean age 69.4 years, 27.5% women.
Intervention
Subcutaneous ziltivekimab 15 mg once monthly (n = 3184).
Comparison
Matching placebo once monthly (n = 3192).
Outcomes
Primary efficacy endpoint was 3-point MACE (death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke): 561 patients (17.6%) with ziltivekimab versus 565 (17.7%) with placebo; hazard ratio 0.99 (95% CI, 0.88 to 1.11; P = 0.84) over a median follow-up of 33 months.

Other results

  • Primary 3-point MACE: 561/3184 (17.6%) ziltivekimab versus 565/3192 (17.7%) placebo; HR 0.99 (95% CI, 0.88 to 1.11; P = 0.84).
  • Target engagement was profound: at 24 months the between-group difference in median percent change was -76 percentage points for free interleukin-6 and -64 percentage points for high-sensitivity CRP.
  • Serious adverse events: 54.6% with ziltivekimab versus 54.8% with placebo.
  • Serious infections: 20.4% with ziltivekimab versus 17.9% with placebo; no effect estimate or P value reported.
  • Median follow-up 33 months with 1126 primary endpoint events, a high event rate (about 17.7% over 33 months) reflecting the chronic kidney disease population.

Strengths

  • Large (6376 patients), double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with hard clinical endpoints and 1126 accrued events, powered to detect a clinically meaningful effect.
  • Unambiguous biological target engagement (76% reduction in free interleukin-6, 64% in hsCRP), so the null result tests the interleukin-6 hypothesis itself rather than inadequate dosing.
  • Enriched enrolment for residual inflammatory risk (hsCRP at least 2 mg/L) plus chronic kidney disease, the population in which the hypothesis was most plausible and event rates are highest.
  • The confidence interval (0.88 to 1.11) is tight enough to exclude any benefit of the magnitude seen with canakinumab in CANTOS or colchicine in LoDoCo2, making this an informative negative trial.
  • Safety was reported with specific attention to serious infections, the expected hazard of interleukin-6 blockade.

Limitations

  • Industry funded (Novo Nordisk); the abstract gives no information on independent endpoint adjudication or data access.
  • The serious infection excess (20.4% versus 17.9%) is reported without an effect estimate, confidence interval or P value, so its magnitude and statistical strength cannot be judged; a 2.5 percentage point absolute excess in a frail chronic kidney disease population is clinically relevant.
  • No breakdown of the individual components of MACE, and no data on heart failure, kidney outcomes or all-cause mortality, which were plausible mechanisms of benefit for an anti-inflammatory in chronic kidney disease.
  • Background therapy (statin, SGLT2 inhibitor, antiplatelet use) and baseline LDL cholesterol and hsCRP distributions are not reported, so it cannot be judged whether modern background treatment left little residual inflammatory risk to modify.
  • Restricted to patients with chronic kidney disease, so the result does not formally exclude benefit of interleukin-6 inhibition in atherosclerosis without chronic kidney disease, although the biological rationale was strongest in this group.
  • Median follow-up of 33 months may be short if anti-inflammatory benefit requires longer exposure, though the completely flat hazard ratio argues against a delayed effect.

Practice implications

Interleukin-6 inhibition should not be pursued for residual inflammatory risk in atherosclerosis with chronic kidney disease: despite near-complete pathway blockade there was no signal of benefit and a numerical excess of serious infections. Combined with the modest canakinumab result, this narrows the clinically useful anti-inflammatory options for residual risk to colchicine, and reinforces that an elevated hsCRP in a patient with chronic kidney disease should prompt optimisation of proven therapies (lipid lowering, SGLT2 inhibition, blood pressure and antithrombotic management) rather than a search for cytokine-directed treatment. Measuring hsCRP remains prognostic but is not, on this evidence, a route to a targeted therapy.

Discontinuing Statins in Older Adults without Cardiovascular Disease.

Rodondi N et al. · PubMed

In 1880 adults aged 70 or older taking statins for primary prevention, stopping the statin was formally noninferior to continuing it on restricted mean event-free survival at 2 years (difference -2.0 weeks; 95% CI, -3.7 to -0.3; P<0.001 for noninferiority), but primary-outcome events were more common after discontinuation (12.7% versus 9.2%), so the result licenses deprescribing only if a small loss of event-free time is acceptable.

Design
International, open-label, randomized controlled noninferiority trial; primary analysis on the between-group difference in restricted mean event-free survival time at 2 years with a noninferiority margin of -5.2 weeks (STREAM, NCT05178420). Funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation.
Population
1880 adults at least 70 years of age taking statins for primary prevention, with at least two chronic conditions and no known cardiovascular disease; mean age 77.5 years, 46.9% women.
Intervention
Discontinuation of statin therapy and other lipid-lowering therapies (n = 938).
Comparison
Continuation of statin therapy and other lipid-lowering therapies (n = 942).
Outcomes
Primary outcome was a composite of nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal ischemic stroke, or death from any cause, whichever occurred first, analysed as the between-group difference in restricted mean event-free survival time at 2 years: -2.0 weeks (95% CI, -3.7 to -0.3; P<0.001 for noninferiority against a margin of -5.2 weeks).

Other results

  • Restricted mean event-free survival time at 2 years: between-group difference -2.0 weeks (95% CI, -3.7 to -0.3; P<0.001 for noninferiority); the margin was -5.2 weeks.
  • Primary-outcome events over a median follow-up of 103 weeks: 119 of 938 (12.7%) in the discontinuation group versus 87 of 942 (9.2%) in the continuation group, an absolute difference of 3.5 percentage points favouring continuation.
  • At least one serious adverse event: 43.0% with discontinuation versus 39.8% with continuation.
  • Mean age 77.5 years, 46.9% women, all with at least two chronic conditions and no known cardiovascular disease.
  • The confidence interval for the primary estimate (-3.7 to -0.3 weeks) excludes zero, so discontinuation caused a small but statistically detectable loss of event-free survival time, albeit less than the prespecified margin.

Strengths

  • Randomized evidence for a deprescribing question that is normally answered by observational data riddled with healthy-continuer bias.
  • Enrolled precisely the population where the decision is hardest: multimorbid adults with a mean age of 77.5 years and no cardiovascular disease, who are systematically excluded from statin efficacy trials.
  • Primary outcome includes all-cause death rather than only cardiovascular endpoints, which is the appropriate framing when competing risks dominate in older patients.
  • Restricted mean survival time is a sensible and transparent metric for older patients, expressing benefit as time gained rather than a hazard ratio under questionable proportional-hazards assumptions.
  • Publicly funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation, with no commercial interest in either result.
  • Both lipid-lowering discontinuation and continuation were defined broadly (statins and other lipid-lowering therapies), matching real deprescribing decisions.

Limitations

  • The noninferiority conclusion depends entirely on accepting a margin of -5.2 weeks of event-free survival over 2 years; the observed -2.0 week difference was statistically significant in favour of continuation, so this is a trial showing a small real harm that was declared tolerable by prior agreement, not a trial showing equivalence.
  • Event counts tell a less reassuring story than the headline: 12.7% versus 9.2% is a relative increase of roughly 38% in myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke or death, and the abstract reports no hazard ratio or confidence interval for that comparison.
  • Open-label design with no blinding of participants or treating clinicians; management intensity and ascertainment of nonfatal events could differ between arms.
  • Follow-up of only 2 years (median 103 weeks) is short relative to the time horizon over which statin benefit accrues, so a 2-year noninferiority finding cannot be extended to patients with longer life expectancy.
  • Baseline LDL cholesterol, statin intensity, duration of prior use, frailty and life-expectancy estimates are not reported, so the subgroup in whom deprescribing is genuinely safe cannot be identified.
  • The composite endpoint is dominated by all-cause death in this age group; without component-level data it is unclear whether the excess events were cardiovascular at all.
  • No data are reported on the putative benefits of deprescribing (muscle symptoms, pill burden, quality of life, cost), so the other side of the trade-off is missing.

Practice implications

Supports discussing statin discontinuation with multimorbid primary-prevention patients over 70, particularly where life expectancy is limited, pill burden is high or adverse effects are troubling: over 2 years the average cost is about 2 weeks of event-free survival. But present it honestly as a small trade-off rather than a free choice, since one additional myocardial infarction, stroke or death occurred for roughly every 29 patients who stopped over about 2 years. For patients with a longer expected horizon, high baseline risk or good tolerance, continuation remains the better-supported default, and the trial says nothing about secondary prevention, where statins should not be stopped.

Intensive Blood-Pressure Lowering in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes.

Santo K et al. · PubMed

In 9476 patients with type 2 diabetes, elevated blood pressure and high cardiovascular risk, a systolic target below 120 mm Hg achieved a 13.6 mm Hg blood-pressure separation but did not significantly reduce major cardiovascular events over a median 4 years (6.0% versus 6.7%; HR 0.90; 95% CI, 0.77 to 1.06; P = 0.20).

Design
Multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial conducted in Brazil (OPTIMAL-DIABETES, NCT04040634), funded by Hospital Israelita Albert Einstein and the Brazilian Ministry of Health.
Population
9476 ethnically diverse patients with elevated blood pressure, type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk; mean age 64.7 +/- 8.2 years, 5871 (62.0%) women.
Intervention
Intensive therapy targeting systolic blood pressure less than 120 mm Hg (n = 4745).
Comparison
Standard therapy targeting systolic blood pressure less than 140 mm Hg (n = 4731).
Outcomes
Primary endpoint was a composite of death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure: 286 patients (6.0%; 1.65 events per 100 person-years) with intensive therapy versus 315 (6.7%; 1.83 events per 100 person-years) with standard therapy; hazard ratio 0.90 (95% CI, 0.77 to 1.06; P = 0.20) over a median follow-up of 4.0 years (IQR, 3.9 to 4.0).

Other results

  • Primary composite: 286/4745 (6.0%; 1.65 per 100 person-years) intensive versus 315/4731 (6.7%; 1.83 per 100 person-years) standard; HR 0.90 (95% CI, 0.77 to 1.06; P = 0.20).
  • Mean systolic blood pressure during follow-up (excluding the first 3 months) was 121.8 +/- 10.8 mm Hg with intensive therapy versus 135.4 +/- 8.2 mm Hg with standard therapy, a separation of about 13.6 mm Hg.
  • Serious adverse events: 706 patients (14.9%) with intensive therapy versus 693 (14.6%) with standard therapy.
  • 601 primary endpoint events accrued over a median follow-up of 4.0 years (IQR, 3.9 to 4.0).
  • Notably high proportion of women (62.0%) in an ethnically diverse Brazilian cohort.

  • Large randomized trial (9476 patients) with a hard clinical composite and successful achievement of the intended 13.6 mm Hg blood-pressure separation, so the intended contrast was genuinely tested.
  • Ethnically diverse population with an unusually high representation of women (62.0%), both groups under-represented in landmark blood-pressure trials.
  • Publicly funded, with no commercial interest in the direction of the result.
  • Uniform and essentially complete follow-up (median 4.0 years, IQR 3.9 to 4.0), indicating excellent retention and administrative censoring at a planned date rather than loss to follow-up.
  • Directly addresses the ACCORD question with a much larger sample and contemporary background diabetes therapy, which makes it an informative negative trial rather than an inconclusive one.

Limitations

  • Open-label design; although the components are relatively objective, hospitalization for unstable angina and heart failure are clinician-driven endpoints susceptible to ascertainment bias.
  • Formally negative but not definitive: the hazard ratio of 0.90 with an upper confidence bound of 1.06 does not exclude a clinically worthwhile 10 to 20% relative reduction, and with only 601 events the trial had limited power for a modest true effect.
  • Event rates were low (about 6% over 4 years) despite enrolment of high-risk patients, which further limits power and suggests the cohort may have been less high-risk than intended.
  • Conducted entirely in Brazil, so health-system, drug-availability and population differences limit generalizability.

Practice implications

Does not support routinely driving systolic blood pressure below 120 mm Hg in patients with type 2 diabetes: a sustained 13.6 mm Hg reduction produced no statistically significant reduction in major cardiovascular events over 4 years, consistent with ACCORD and at odds with the recent BPROAD result. A target below 130 mm Hg remains a defensible default, with more aggressive lowering reserved for patients who tolerate it easily or who have an additional indication such as prior stroke, where the companion OPTIMAL-STROKE trial did show benefit. The point estimate of 0.90 leaves room for a modest real benefit, so this is a reason not to pursue a sub-120 target aggressively rather than evidence that lower is harmful.

605 patients older than 18 years with acute myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock, enrolled between 1 August 2018 and 19 January 2024; 137 (23%) were female.

Intravenous cangrelor 30 microgram/kg bolus followed by an infusion of 4 microgram/kg per min for at least 2 h (n = 298).
Comparison
Crushed oral ticagrelor 180 mg loading dose (n = 307).
Outcomes
Primary laboratory endpoint was a platelet reactivity index of less than 50% at the end of primary PCI: achieved in 56 (100%) of 56 tested cangrelor patients versus 15 (22%) of 68 tested ticagrelor patients (p<0.0001).   • Primary laboratory endpoint (platelet reactivity index <50% at end of PCI): 56/56 (100%) cangrelor versus 15/68 (22%) ticagrelor; p<0.0001. It was assessed in only 56 (19%) and 68 (22%) of patients respectively.

  • Non-inferiority was not demonstrated because the upper bound of the 90% CI (+3 percentage points) exceeded the prespecified 1 percentage point margin, even though the point estimate favoured cangrelor.
  • Major bleeding at 30 days: 19 (6%) of 298 with cangrelor versus 16 (5%) of 307 with ticagrelor.

  • The first randomised comparison of P2Y12 inhibitor strategies specifically in infarct-related cardiogenic shock, a population routinely excluded from antiplatelet trials despite having the greatest pharmacokinetic problem.

  • Paired a pharmacodynamic endpoint with a clinical endpoint, so the mechanistic claim (cangrelor works where oral absorption fails) and the clinical claim are separable.
  • Non-commercial funding from national and European public sources.
  • Pragmatically relevant comparator: crushed ticagrelor is the workaround most centres already use in intubated or shocked patients.

  • The 1 percentage point non-inferiority margin is indefensible in a population with a 30-day event rate near 40%; no trial of this size could have met it, so the failed non-inferiority test says more about the design than about cangrelor.
  • The primary laboratory endpoint was measured in only about 20% of patients (56 and 68), introducing serious selection bias: platelet function testing was likely feasible in the more stable survivors, and the 100% versus 22% contrast should be read as indicative rather than exact.
  • Underpowered for clinical outcomes: 605 patients and 238 events give a 90% CI spanning -10 to +3 percentage points, consistent with a worthwhile benefit or a small harm.
  • A 90% rather than 95% confidence interval is reported for the clinical endpoint, which understates uncertainty relative to conventional reporting.
  • Conducted in five European countries at centres equipped for primary PCI in shock, and the use of mechanical circulatory support, revascularisation completeness and shock severity (SCAI stage, lactate) are not reported, limiting interpretation and generalisability.
  • No breakdown of the composite into death, myocardial infarction and stroke, so it is unclear whether the numerical 3 percentage point difference reflects mortality or ischaemic events.
  • Cangrelor requires a continuous infusion and a transition strategy to an oral agent that is not described here, and the trial gives no data on stent thrombosis, the outcome the pharmacodynamic advantage should most plausibly affect.

Practice implications

Confirms what the pharmacology predicted: crushed ticagrelor achieves adequate platelet inhibition at the end of PCI in only about a fifth of patients in cardiogenic shock, whereas cangrelor does so reliably, with no excess major bleeding (6% versus 5%). Clinical outcomes were numerically better with cangrelor but the trial cannot prove it, so this is a reasonable basis for preferring intravenous cangrelor in intubated or shocked patients undergoing primary PCI on mechanistic grounds while recognising that hard-outcome benefit remains unproven. Plan the transition to an oral P2Y12 inhibitor explicitly, and treat the failed non-inferiority test as an artefact of a 1 percentage point margin rather than evidence against cangrelor.

  • Prespecified principal subgroup with low-voltage areas present (n = 268): recurrence 68 of 138 (49.3%) versus 90 of 130 (69.2%); HR 0.77 (95% CI, 0.66 to 0.91; P = .002).
  • In that subgroup, recurrence over 18 months was 37.0% versus 56.2% (HR 0.77; 95% CI, 0.64 to 0.92), and atrial fibrillation burden was also lower.
  • In patients without low-voltage areas: recurrence 56 of 113 (49.6%) versus 51 of 113 (45.1%); HR 1.09 (95% CI, 0.90 to 1.32), i.e. no benefit and a numerically unfavourable direction.
  • Procedure duration and total ablation delivery time were longer with adjunctive low-voltage area ablation; safety outcomes were similar between groups.

Strengths

  • Randomized, multicentre (14 Canadian centres) test of a widely promoted but unproven adjunctive ablation strategy, with single-blind design and an endpoint assessed beyond a 90-day blanking period.
  • The low-voltage-area-present analysis was prespecified as the principal subgroup rather than discovered after the fact, and the biological rationale (ablate substrate only where substrate exists) predicts exactly the interaction observed.
  • The contrast between the subgroup with low-voltage areas (HR 0.77) and without (HR 1.09) is internally coherent and makes a chance finding less likely than a conventional subgroup result.
  • Clinically meaningful secondary outcomes were reported, including 18-month recurrence, atrial fibrillation burden, repeat ablation and procedural metrics.
  • Honest reporting of the procedural cost of the strategy: longer procedure and ablation delivery times with no safety penalty.
  • Long follow-up (mean 21 months, up to 36 months) for an ablation trial, which matters because late recurrence is common in persistent atrial fibrillation.

Limitations

  • The trial missed its primary endpoint; the overall result is formally negative, and the positive finding comes from a subgroup comprising just over half the cohort, so it is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.
  • Patients without low-voltage areas could not benefit from the intervention by definition, which dilutes the overall estimate and means the primary endpoint was arguably the wrong question; this is a design weakness rather than a biological null.
  • Enrichment was impossible prospectively because low-voltage area status is determined during the procedure, so the subgroup is post-randomization in timing even though prespecified in analysis.
  • Single-blind: patients were blinded but operators were not, and recurrence detection depends on monitoring intensity, which is not described in the abstract.
  • The low-voltage area subgroup contains only 268 patients and 158 events, and the reported confidence intervals are implausibly narrow for a subgroup of that size relative to the overall analysis, which warrants checking against the full paper.
  • Definition and voltage threshold for low-voltage areas, mapping method, acute procedural endpoints and the proportion achieving durable isolation are not reported, and these determine whether the result is reproducible in other labs.
  • Modified intention-to-treat analysis restricted to patients who received the intervention departs from strict intention-to-treat and can favour the intervention.

Practice implications

Does not support routinely adding low-voltage area ablation to pulmonary vein isolation for everyone with persistent atrial fibrillation: in patients without low-voltage areas it lengthened the procedure with no benefit. It does support mapping for substrate and ablating it when low-voltage areas are found, where the prespecified subgroup showed an absolute reduction in recurrence of about 20 percentage points at a cost of longer procedure time and no excess complications. Treat this as a reasonable, mechanistically coherent strategy to adopt selectively while awaiting a trial that enrols only patients with documented low-voltage areas.

Long-Term Survival After Initial Invasive or Conservative Management of Stable Coronary Disease in ISCHEMIA-EXTEND.

Hochman JS et al. · PubMed

After a median 9 years and 1071 deaths among 5179 ISCHEMIA participants with chronic coronary disease and moderate-to-severe ischemia, an initial invasive strategy did not improve 11-year all-cause mortality (25.8% versus 26.9%; adjusted HR 0.97; 95% CI, 0.86 to 1.09), with a signal of fewer cardiovascular deaths (AHR 0.88; 95% CI, 0.75 to 1.02) offset by more noncardiovascular deaths (AHR 1.18; 95% CI, 0.96 to 1.45).

Design
Prespecified long-term follow-up study of the international ISCHEMIA randomized trial (320 sites in 37 countries, randomization from August 2012, follow-up through December 2025), analysed by intention to treat using cumulative incidence, Cox and Fine-Gray models, bayesian analysis and restricted mean survival time (ISCHEMIA-EXTEND, NCT04894877).
Population
5179 participants with chronic coronary disease, moderate or severe ischemia, left ventricular ejection fraction of 35% or more, and no left main coronary artery disease, unacceptable angina or recent acute coronary syndrome; median age 64 years (IQR, 58-70), 23% female, 42% with diabetes, median ejection fraction 60% (IQR, 55%-65%).
Intervention
Initial invasive strategy: guideline-directed medical therapy plus coronary angiography and revascularization when feasible.
Comparison
Initial conservative strategy: guideline-directed medical therapy alone, with invasive management reserved for failure of medical therapy.
Outcomes
All-cause mortality at 11 years was 25.8% with the invasive strategy versus 26.9% with the conservative strategy (estimated difference -1.0 percentage point [95% CI, -4.2 to 2.1]; adjusted hazard ratio 0.97 [95% CI, 0.86 to 1.09]). Cardiovascular mortality at 11 years was 15.8% versus 17.1% (difference -1.4 percentage points [95% CI, -4.0 to 1.3]; AHR 0.88 [95% CI, 0.75 to 1.02]); noncardiovascular mortality 9.1% versus 8.1% (difference 1.0 percentage point [95% CI, -0.9 to 2.9]; AHR 1.18 [95% CI, 0.96 to 1.45]).

Other results

  • All-cause 11-year mortality: 25.8% invasive versus 26.9% conservative; estimated difference -1.0 percentage point (95% CI, -4.2 to 2.1); adjusted HR 0.97 (95% CI, 0.86 to 1.09).
  • Cardiovascular mortality at 11 years: 15.8% versus 17.1%; difference -1.4 percentage points (95% CI, -4.0 to 1.3); AHR 0.88 (95% CI, 0.75 to 1.02).
  • Noncardiovascular mortality at 11 years: 9.1% versus 8.1%; difference 1.0 percentage point (95% CI, -0.9 to 2.9); AHR 1.18 (95% CI, 0.96 to 1.45).
  • Bayesian analysis: posterior probability of more than a 1 percentage point survival benefit with the invasive strategy was only 18%.
  • Eleven-year restricted mean survival time difference was 14 days (95% CI, -41 to 70 days).
  • 1071 deaths occurred over a median 9-year follow-up (range 1 day to 13.4 years), 782 of them during extended follow-up: 672 cardiovascular, 359 noncardiovascular, 40 unclassified.

Strengths

  • Answers the question the original trial could not: with 1071 deaths over a median 9 years, this is now adequately powered for mortality, whereas the parent trial's 3.2-year follow-up was not.
  • Intention-to-treat analysis of a large (5179 patients), international, randomized cohort, preserving the causal interpretation despite the long extension.
  • Multiple complementary analytic approaches (cumulative incidence, Cox, Fine-Gray competing-risk, bayesian and restricted mean survival time) converge on the same answer, which greatly strengthens confidence in the null.
  • Reports the bayesian posterior probability (18% for more than a 1 percentage point benefit) and restricted mean survival time difference (14 days), both of which communicate clinically interpretable magnitudes rather than just a non-significant P value.
  • Separates cardiovascular from noncardiovascular mortality, exposing the offsetting pattern that a single all-cause estimate would hide.
  • Extended follow-up was prespecified and separately registered rather than opportunistic.

Limitations

  • During extended follow-up, treatment is no longer randomized in practice: patients in the conservative arm were revascularized for symptoms over time and adherence to medical therapy drifted, so this measures initial strategy rather than sustained contrast, biasing toward the null.
  • Only vital status was collected in the extension; nonfatal myocardial infarction, angina, quality of life and repeat revascularization, where ISCHEMIA originally showed differences, are not available for the long term.
  • The divergent cause-specific signals (cardiovascular AHR 0.88, noncardiovascular AHR 1.18) are both non-significant and individually fragile; the suggestion that invasive management trades cardiovascular for noncardiovascular death is hypothesis-generating and susceptible to cause-of-death misclassification, with 40 deaths unclassified.
  • The enrolled population excluded left main disease, ejection fraction below 35%, unacceptable angina and recent acute coronary syndrome, so the result cannot be extended to those groups, in whom revascularization is indicated.
  • Medical therapy and revascularization techniques have evolved since 2012 enrolment, and crossover rates during extended follow-up are not reported here.
  • Completeness of long-term vital status ascertainment across 37 countries is not stated, and differential ascertainment would affect mortality estimates.

Practice implications

Settles the long-running question of whether routine revascularization prolongs life in chronic coronary disease with preserved ejection fraction and no left main disease: over 11 years it does not, with a restricted mean survival difference of 14 days and only an 18% posterior probability of even a 1 percentage point benefit. Use this when counselling patients who equate a stent with longevity: revascularization in this setting is for symptoms and quality of life, and the decision should rest on angina burden and preference while optimising medical therapy. The findings do not apply to left main disease, reduced ejection fraction, refractory angina or acute coronary syndromes.

Long-Term Survival After Initial Invasive or Conservative Management of Stable Coronary Disease in Patients With Advanced Chronic Kidney Disease: ISCHEMIA-CKD EXTEND.

Bangalore S et al. · PubMed

Over a median 8 years and 429 deaths among 777 patients with advanced chronic kidney disease and moderate-to-severe ischemia, an initial invasive strategy gave no long-term survival benefit over guideline-directed medical therapy alone (8-year all-cause mortality 57.4% versus 56.8%; adjusted HR 1.05; 95% CI, 0.87 to 1.28), with the point estimates and a -99 day restricted mean survival difference all favouring the conservative strategy.

Design
Prespecified extended vital-status follow-up of the randomized ISCHEMIA-CKD trial; long-term survival ascertained by site contact, health record review and centralized death database searches (ISCHEMIA-CKD EXTEND, NCT01985360).
Population
777 participants randomized in ISCHEMIA-CKD with moderate or severe myocardial ischemia and advanced chronic kidney disease (388 invasive, 389 conservative); vital status retention among survivors was 95%.
Intervention
Initial invasive strategy: coronary angiography and revascularization when feasible, added to guideline-directed medical therapy (n = 388).
Comparison
Initial conservative strategy: guideline-directed medical therapy alone, with invasive procedures reserved for failure of medical therapy (n = 389).
Outcomes
Primary outcome for the extended phase was all-cause mortality: cumulative 8-year mortality 57.4% with the invasive strategy versus 56.8% with the conservative strategy; adjusted hazard ratio 1.05 (95% CI, 0.87 to 1.28). Cardiovascular mortality at 8 years was 40.3% versus 38.4%; adjusted hazard ratio 1.06 (95% CI, 0.84 to 1.34).

Other results

  • All-cause mortality at 8 years: 57.4% invasive versus 56.8% conservative; adjusted HR 1.05 (95% CI, 0.87 to 1.28).
  • Cardiovascular mortality at 8 years: 40.3% versus 38.4%; adjusted HR 1.06 (95% CI, 0.84 to 1.34).
  • Restricted mean survival time: 1941 days (1834-2047) with the invasive strategy versus 1990 days (1885-2094) with the conservative strategy; adjusted difference -99 days (-245 to 46).
  • Bayesian posterior probability of at least a 1 percentage point lower versus higher death with the invasive strategy: 28% versus 52%; for cardiovascular mortality, 23% versus 56%.
  • 429 deaths (55% of the cohort) over a median 8 years, with 95% vital status retention among survivors.

Strengths

  • Long-term randomized follow-up of a population almost always excluded from coronary revascularization trials, where management decisions are made on extrapolation and registry data.
  • Excellent ascertainment: 95% vital status retention among survivors, achieved by combining site contact with health record and centralized death database searches.
  • A very high event rate (429 deaths, 55% mortality) means that despite the modest sample size the mortality comparison is reasonably informative.
  • Reports restricted mean survival time (-99 days) and bayesian posterior probabilities alongside hazard ratios, giving a clinically interpretable sense of magnitude and direction rather than only a non-significant P value.
  • All estimates point consistently in the same direction (no benefit, if anything slight harm from the invasive strategy), which is more persuasive than a single null hazard ratio.
  • Prespecified extension of a registered randomized trial, with cause-specific mortality reported separately.

Limitations

  • Modest sample size (777) means the confidence interval for all-cause mortality (0.87 to 1.28) cannot exclude a worthwhile 13% relative benefit or a 28% relative harm.
  • Only vital status was collected in the extension: nonfatal myocardial infarction, dialysis initiation, angina, quality of life and repeat revascularization are unavailable, so the full long-term trade-off cannot be assessed.
  • Treatment is no longer randomized during extended follow-up; crossover to revascularization in the conservative arm and attrition of medical therapy adherence are not reported and would bias toward the null.
  • Competing risk of death from kidney disease and other noncardiovascular causes is overwhelming in this population (57% mortality at 8 years), which structurally limits any strategy's ability to improve survival and should temper generalization to patients with less advanced kidney disease.
  • Baseline kidney function strata, dialysis status at randomization, and whether procedural contrast exposure caused measurable kidney function loss are not reported here, although contrast nephropathy is the central concern with an invasive strategy in this group.
  • Mortality in the parent trial was already similar at 2.2 years, so this extension confirms rather than changes the main message; it does not resolve whether a subgroup benefits.

Practice implications

Supports a conservative, medically managed default for patients with advanced chronic kidney disease and stable coronary disease with moderate-to-severe ischemia: eight years of randomized follow-up show no survival advantage from routine angiography and revascularization, and every estimate including restricted mean survival time trends the other way. Given the competing risks and procedural hazards of contrast exposure and vascular access in this population, reserve an invasive strategy for refractory symptoms, acute coronary syndromes or anatomy not represented in the trial, and focus on optimising medical therapy and kidney-protective treatment.

Left Atrial Appendage Closure in Atrial Fibrillation Patients With High Risk of Stroke and Bleeding: An Individual Participant Data Meta-Analysis of Randomized Trials.

Skurk C et al. · PubMed

Pooling individual participant data from the only two randomized trials of left atrial appendage closure against contemporary medical therapy in genuinely high-risk atrial fibrillation (1290 patients, median age 77.3 years), closure showed no efficacy advantage (21.9% versus 20.7%; adjusted HR 0.98; 95% CI, 0.77 to 1.24; P = 0.842) and no bleeding advantage by 3 years, with an excess of major bleeding in the first year (9.4% versus 6.0%; absolute risk difference 3.4 percentage points; 95% CI, 0.4 to 6.3).

Design
Individual participant data meta-analysis of randomized trials comparing percutaneous left atrial appendage closure with contemporary medical therapy in populations with a trial-level mean CHA2DS2-VASc score of at least 4; databases and registries searched through 5 August 2026; one-stage common-effect Cox models stratified by trial and adjusted for age, sex, CHA2DS2-VASc and HAS-BLED scores, with a flexible time-varying Cox model for major bleeding (PROSPERO CRD420261335972).
Population
1290 participants from two trials, CLOSURE-AF and PRAGUE-17 (647 assigned to closure, 643 to control); median age 77.3 years (Q1-Q3, 71.4 to 81.9), 809 (62.7%) men.
Intervention
Percutaneous left atrial appendage closure (n = 647).
Comparison
Contemporary medical therapy (n = 643).
Outcomes
Co-primary outcomes were time to first stroke, systemic embolism, or cardiovascular/unknown death (efficacy): 142 closure participants (21.9%) versus 133 controls (20.7%); adjusted HR 0.98 (95% CI, 0.77 to 1.24; P = 0.842); and time to first major bleeding (safety): 86 (13.3%) versus 80 (12.4%); adjusted HR 1.03 (95% CI, 0.75 to 1.39; P = 0.874).

Other results

  • Primary efficacy composite: 142/647 (21.9%) closure versus 133/643 (20.7%) control; adjusted HR 0.98 (95% CI, 0.77 to 1.24; P = 0.842).
  • Major bleeding overall: 86/647 (13.3%) closure versus 80/643 (12.4%) control; adjusted HR 1.03 (95% CI, 0.75 to 1.39; P = 0.874).
  • The bleeding hazard varied over time (P for treatment-by-time interaction < 0.001): cumulative major bleeding 9.4% with closure versus 6.0% with control at 1 year (absolute risk difference 3.4 percentage points; 95% CI, 0.4 to 6.3), narrowing to 13.2% versus 12.1% at 3 years (absolute risk difference 1.1 percentage points; 95% CI, -2.8 to 5.0).
  • Only two trials met the inclusion criterion of a trial-level mean CHA2DS2-VASc score of at least 4: CLOSURE-AF and PRAGUE-17.
  • Event rates were high in both arms (about 21% for the efficacy composite), consistent with the elderly, high-risk population.

Strengths

  • Individual participant data rather than published summary estimates, allowing a one-stage Cox analysis stratified by trial with adjustment for age, sex, CHA2DS2-VASc and HAS-BLED, which is the strongest available synthesis design.
  • Restricted a priori to trials enrolling genuinely high-risk patients (mean CHA2DS2-VASc at least 4), so it addresses the population in which left atrial appendage closure is actually offered rather than pooling heterogeneous trials with low-risk comparators.
  • Prospectively registered protocol (PROSPERO) with a prespecified search date and co-primary efficacy and safety outcomes, so both sides of the trade-off were tested.
  • The time-varying bleeding analysis is the right methodological choice and reveals a clinically important pattern that a single hazard ratio conceals: bleeding is front-loaded after implantation.
  • Reports absolute risk differences with confidence intervals at 1 and 3 years, which is far more useful for shared decision-making than hazard ratios alone.

Limitations

  • Only two trials and 1290 participants: this is a small evidence base, and the confidence intervals (efficacy 0.77 to 1.24; bleeding 0.75 to 1.39) cannot exclude a meaningful benefit or harm in either direction.
  • The two included trials differ in comparator and population (PRAGUE-17 used direct oral anticoagulants in anticoagulation-eligible patients, CLOSURE-AF compared with medical therapy in patients with contraindications), so a common-effect model may pool clinically different questions.
  • A common-effect rather than random-effects model assumes a single underlying treatment effect, which is hard to justify across trials with different comparators, device generations and post-procedural antithrombotic regimens.
  • The central claim about the time-varying bleeding hazard rests on a subgroup-in-time analysis; the later estimates are imprecise, as the authors acknowledge, and the 3-year difference of 1.1 percentage points (95% CI, -2.8 to 5.0) is compatible with no benefit.
  • Post-procedural antithrombotic regimens, which largely determine early bleeding after closure, are not described here but differ between trials and have since changed in practice.
  • Excludes the recent trials in anticoagulation-eligible populations (OPTION, CHAMPION-AF) by design, so the synthesis does not speak to that increasingly common indication.
  • Follow-up extends only to about 3 years, too short to capture the point at which avoiding lifelong anticoagulation might finally pay off in bleeding terms.

Practice implications

The best randomized evidence in high-risk atrial fibrillation shows left atrial appendage closure neither prevents more strokes, systemic emboli or cardiovascular deaths than contemporary medical therapy nor reduces major bleeding within 3 years, and it costs an absolute excess of about 3.4 percentage points of major bleeding in the first year because of the mandated post-implant antithrombotic regimen. Anticoagulation should remain the default where it is feasible, with closure reserved for patients in whom anticoagulation is genuinely unsafe or unsustainable, and the conversation should include the front-loaded early bleeding risk and the fact that any late bleeding benefit is as yet unproven.
